1 元丰间，彧先公为右史，神考遣使治楚州新河，面戒之曰：「东南不惯兴大役，卿且为朕爱惜兵民。」大哉王言，简而有体。

During the Yuangong era, Yuxian gong was a Right Historiographer. Shen kao sent an envoy to manage the new river in Chuzhou and personally warned him, saying: "The southeast is not accustomed to undertaking large-scale projects; you should for me cherish the soldiers and civilians." How great are the words of a monarch! They are concise yet dignified.

2 元丰六年冬祀，先公导驾，既进辇，辇中忘设衾褥，遽取未至。上觉之，乃指顾问他事。少选褥至，遂升辇。以故官吏无罪，圣度如此。

During the winter sacrifice in the sixth year of Yuangong, Xian gong led the imperial procession. After proceeding to the palanquin, it was forgotten to place quilts and mats inside; they were hastily fetched but had not yet arrived. The emperor noticed this and deliberately pointed and asked about other matters instead. After a short while, the mats arrived, and he then ascended into the palanquin. For this reason, no officials were punished; such was the magnanimity of the sage ruler.

3 舅氏胡宗尧，嘉佑初引见改官，举将十七员，仁宗问其家世，或奏枢密使胡宿之子，即有旨「更候一任回改官」。时又有因失人死罪连坐，于条合展举将员改次第等官，上宣谕未令改官，凡三引见，几十馀年。大臣或以为言，上曰：「此人曾杀朕百姓，不可改官。」

My maternal uncle Hu Zongyao, during the early Jiayou era, was summoned for an audience to change his official post. He had recommended seventeen military officers. Emperor Renzong asked about his family background, and someone reported that he was the son of Hu Su, a former Director of the Military Council (Shumishi). Immediately, an imperial decree stated: "Wait one more term before changing your official post." At that time, there were also cases where officials were implicated in capital punishment due to the death of someone under their charge and were thus required by regulation to be reclassified among military officers according to certain procedures. However, the emperor issued an edict stating that they should not be reassigned; altogether, he summoned them for audiences three times, spanning nearly ten years. Some high ministers suggested this matter to the emperor, who said: "This person once killed my people; he cannot be reassigned to another official post."

4 三省俱在禁中，元丰间移尚书省于大内西，切近西角楼，人呼为「新省」。崇宁间，又移于大内西南，其地遂号「旧省」，以建左右班直。或云，旧省不利宰相，自创省至废，蔡确、王圭、吕公著、司马光、吕大防、刘挚、苏颂、章敦、曾布更九相，唯子容居位日浅，亦谪罢，馀不以存没，或贬广南，或贬散官。

The Three Provinces were originally all located within the imperial palace. During the Yuangong era, the Secretariat was moved to the west of the Grand Inner Palace, close to the western corner tower; people called it the "Xin Sheng" (New Province). During the Chongning era, it was moved again to the southwest of the Grand Inner Palace; that location then became known as the "Jiu Sheng" (Old Province), in order to establish the Left and Right Attendant Divisions. Some say that the Old Province was inauspicious for chancellors. From its establishment to its abolition, nine chancellors succeeded one another: Cai Que, Wang Gui, Lü Gongzhu, Sima Guang, Lü Dafang, Liu Zhi, Su Song, Zhang Dun, and Zeng Bu; only Zi Rong held office briefly before also being exiled or dismissed. The rest were either demoted to Guangnan or appointed as minor officials, regardless of whether they were alive or dead.

5 祖宗故事：宰相呼相公；节度使带开府仪同三司，元丰官制前带同中书门下平章事，亦呼相公，谓之使相；三公正真相之任，呼公相，尚书改令厅为公相厅。蔡京首以太师为公相，其子攸自淮康军节度使除开府仪同三司，遂父呼公相，子呼相公。时传京父子入侍曲宴，上云：「相公公相子。」京对云：「人主主人翁。」际遇之盛如此。

Ancestral regulations: chancellors were addressed as Xianggong. Military governors (Jiedushi) who held the title Kai Fu Yitong Sansi before the Yuangong official system also bore the title Tong Zhongshu Menxia Pingzhangshi, and were similarly called Xianggong; they were referred to as "Shixiang." When the Three Provinces actually assumed the duties of chancellor, they were addressed as Gongxiang. The Secretariat changed its Hall of Commands (Lingting) into the Hall of Gongxiang. Cai Jing was the first to hold the title of Taishi (Grand Tutor) and be addressed as Gongxiang; his son You, while serving as Jiedushi of Huikang Army, was appointed Kai Fu Yitong Sanshi. Thus, the father was called Gongxiang, and the son Xianggong. At that time, it was said that Cai Jing and his father entered to attend a private banquet. The emperor remarked: "The son of Gongxiang is Xianggong." Jing replied: "The Son of Heaven is the master of the house." Thus great was their favor and fortune.

6 宰相礼绝庶官，都堂自京官以上则坐，选人立白事；见于私第，虽选人亦坐，盖客礼也。唯两制以上点茶汤，入脚床子，寒月有火垆，暑月有扇，谓之「事事有」，庶官只点茶，谓之「事事无」。

Chancellors observed a strict etiquette that set them apart from other officials. In the Grand Hall, those holding official posts at the level of Jingguan or higher were allowed to sit; candidates for office (xuanshi) had to stand and report their business. However, when meetings took place in private residences, even xuanshi were permitted to sit, as this was considered a gesture of hospitality toward guests. Only officials of the rank of Neishi or higher were served tea and soup, given foot warmers (jiao chuangzi), a brazier in cold months, and fans in hot months; this was called "Shi Shi You" ("Everything is Provided"). Common officials received only tea, which was referred to as "Shi Shi Wu" ("Nothing is Provided").

7 茶见于唐时，味苦而转甘，晚采者为茗。今世俗客至则啜茶，去则啜汤。汤取药材甘香者屑之，或温或凉，未有不用甘草者，此俗遍天下。先公使辽，辽人相见，其俗先点汤，后点茶。至饮会亦先水饮，然后品味以进。但欲与中国相反，本无义理。

Tea appeared during the Tang dynasty; its taste is bitter but turns sweet, and late-harvested leaves are called Ming. In present society, when guests arrive, they are offered tea to drink; when they leave, they are given soup to drink. The soup is made by grinding sweet and fragrant medicinal ingredients into powder, served either warm or cool, but none is prepared without licorice. This custom is widespread throughout the world. Xian gong was sent as an envoy to Liao, and when meeting with Liao officials, their custom was first to serve soup and then tea. At drinking gatherings, they also followed the same order: water or soup was served first, and only after that were various dishes and delicacies presented in sequence. They merely wished to do the opposite of what was customary in China, but this had no basis in reason or principle.

8 朝，辨色始入，前此集禁门外。宰执以下，皆用白纸糊烛灯一枚，长柄揭之马前，书官位于其上，欲识马所在也。朝时自四鼓，旧城诸门启关放入，都下人谓「四更时，朝马动，朝士至」者，以烛笼相围绕聚首，谓之「火城」。宰执最后至，至则「火城」灭烛。大臣自从官及亲王驸马，皆有位次，在皇城外仗舍，谓之待漏院，不与庶官同处。「火城」每位有翰林司官给酒果，以供朝臣，酒绝佳，果实皆不可咀嚼，欲其久存。先公与蔡元度尝以寒月至待漏院，卒前白有羊肉酒，探腰间布囊，取一纸角，视之，也。问其故，云「恐寒冻难解，故怀之」。自是止令供清酒。

The court session began at dawn; prior to this, officials assembled outside the palace gates. Chancellors and high-ranking officials down to lower ranks each used a single candle made by wrapping white paper around it; these candles were mounted on long poles held in front of their horses, with the official's position written on them so that one could recognize where the horse was located. The court session began as early as the fourth gong (two hours before dawn). At this time, all the gates of the old city were opened to allow entry. People in the capital said: "At the fourth geng, the morning horse moves and the officials arrive," referring to how they gathered together, surrounded by candle lanterns; this formation was called a "Huocheng" (Fire City). The chancellors and high ministers arrived last. When they arrived, the "Fire City" extinguished its candles. High-ranking ministers, from their attendants and close officials up to imperial princes and marquises by marriage, all had designated positions in the outer palace area within the imperial city; these quarters were called Dailouyuan (the Waiting-for-the-Dawn Courtyard), and they did not reside there together with common officials. Each position within the "Fire City" was supplied with wine and fruits by Hanlin officials, to be offered to court ministers. The wine was exceptionally fine, but the fruits were all inedible, meant only to last a long time. Xian gong and Cai Yuandu once arrived at the Dailouyuan in winter, when a subordinate suddenly reported that there was mutton and wine available. He reached into his waist pouch, took out a scrap of paper, looked at it, and said: "Yes." When asked the reason, he replied: "I was afraid that in the cold it would be difficult to open, so I kept it close." From then on, they merely ordered clear wine to be provided.

9 本朝置大宗正寺治宗室，濮邸最亲，嗣王最贵，于属籍最尊，世世知大宗正事。自宗晟迄宗汉，皆安懿王子，兄弟相继，宗字行尽死，诸孙仲字行复嗣爵判宗正寺，人人谨厚练敏，宗子率从其教诲。崇宁初，分置敦宗院于三京，以居踈冗，选宗子之贤者莅治。院中或有尊行，治之者颇以为难。令郯初除南京敦宗院，入对，上问所以治宗子之略，对曰：「长于臣者以国法治之，幼于臣者以家法治之。」上称善，进职而遣之。令郯既至，宗子率教，未尝扰人，京邑甚有赖焉。

In our dynasty, the Dazongzheng Si (Office of the Grand Director of Ancestral Affairs) was established to oversee the imperial clan. The Pu Di lineage was the closest in relation, and among them, the successor prince held the highest status; within the family register, they occupied the most exalted position, and generation after generation served as directors of the Dazongzheng office. From Zong Sheng to Zong Han, they were all sons of Prince Anyi; brothers succeeded one another in office. When the generation with the character "Zong" died out, their grandsons from the "Zhong" generational name resumed the hereditary title and served as magistrates of the Zongzheng Si. Each was careful, sincere, experienced, and perceptive, and imperial clan members generally followed their teachings and guidance. During the early Chongning era, the Dunzong Yuan (Institute for Honoring the Clan) was established in the three capitals to house distant and less prominent members of the imperial family; virtuous descendants of the clan were selected to oversee its administration. Sometimes there were senior relatives within the institute, and those in charge found it quite difficult to manage them. When Ling Tan was first appointed as director of the Dunzong Yuan in Nanjing, he presented himself for an audience. The emperor asked him about his strategy for managing members of the imperial clan, and he replied: "Those older than I am will be governed by state law; those younger than I am will be managed according to family rules." The emperor praised his answer and promoted him before sending him on his way. Ling Tan, upon arriving at the institute, successfully guided the imperial descendants, who all followed his teachings without causing any disturbance; the capital greatly relied on him.

10 嗣濮王宗晟，伯仲第十二，英庙亲兄也。元丰间，神考将诣睦亲宅浇奠近亲，嗣王欲邀车驾幸旧邸，会日逼不及造朝。故事：戚里近属，许献时新，即于东华门投进。时邸中无新果，求得丁香荔枝数百枚函之，附短奏云：「来日乞诣安懿王影堂烧香。」进入，上果喜曰：「十二自来晓事。」即降处分，暨至濮邸，望见祠貌，下辇去伞，洒泪而入。既已，延见近族，慰劳诸父，加恩各迁使相郡王。

Prince Zong Sheng, successor to the Pu 王府, was the twelfth among his brothers and an elder brother of Emperor Yingmiao. During the Yuangong era, Emperor Shen kao was about to visit Mupin Zhai (the residence for imperial relatives) to pay respects at the tomb of a close relative. The successor prince wished to invite the emperor's procession to visit his former mansion; however, time was too short to make proper court preparations in advance. According to ancestral regulations: close relatives of the imperial family were permitted to present seasonal offerings, which could be submitted directly at Donghua Gate. At that time, the mansion had no fresh fruits available. They managed to obtain several hundred cloves of fragrant lychees and packed them in a box, attaching a brief memorial which read: "Tomorrow I humbly request permission to visit the shrine of Prince Anyi to burn incense." When presented, the emperor was pleased and said: "The twelfth prince has always been well-mannered and considerate." Imperial edicts were immediately issued. Upon arriving at the Pu Di, when he caught sight of the ancestral shrine, he dismounted from his palanquin and removed the canopy, weeping as he entered. Afterward, he received a private audience with close relatives, comforted his uncles, and bestowed favors upon them, promoting each to the rank of Shixiang Junwang (military governor-junior prince).

11 嘉王颢，裕陵亲弟也，好读书。元丰间，数上疏论政事，记室或谏之曰：「大王为天子弟，无狗马声色之好，游心方册，固是盛德，而数干廷议，非所以安太后也。」王矍然亦悟。尔后惟求医书，与其僚讲汤液方论而已。朝廷果贤其好古，降诏襃谕。至今医家有《嘉王集方》。

Prince Jia Hao was the younger brother of Yuling and loved reading. During the Yuangong era, he submitted memorials several times to discuss state affairs. His secretaries once advised him: "Your Highness is a son of Heaven; you have no fondness for dogs, horses, music, or beauty, and instead devote your mind to books—this is indeed a great virtue. However, frequently intervening in court deliberations is not conducive to the peace of the Empress Dowager." The prince was startled and realized his mistake. From then on, he sought only medical texts and discussed prescriptions and theories of herbal medicine with his attendants. The court indeed regarded his appreciation for ancient learning as commendable and issued an imperial edict to praise and commend him. To this day, medical practitioners have a collection known as the Jia Wang Ji Fang (The Prince Jia's Collection of Prescriptions).

12 熙宁间，始命宗室应科举；大观间，内臣有赴殿试者；政和八年，帝子亦赴殿试。宗子及第，始于令铄；内臣及第，始于梁师成；亲王及第，始于嘉王楷。故事：有官人应举谓之锁厅，例不作廷魁。戊戌榜，嘉王第一人，登仕郎王昴第二人，颜天选第三人，上宣谕：「嘉王楷有司考在第一，不欲以魁天下，以第二人为榜首。」锁厅人作廷魁，自王昴始。

During the Xining era, imperial clan members were first ordered to participate in the imperial examinations; during the Daguan era, palace attendants also took part in the palace examination. In the eighth year of Zhenghe, even the emperor's sons participated in the palace examination as well. Imperial clan members passing the jinshi examination began with Ling Shuo; palace attendants passing it began with Liang Shicheng; and a close imperial prince passing it began with Prince Jia Kai. According to ancestral regulations: officials taking the examination were called "suoting" (locked-hall candidates), and by convention, they did not become champions of the palace examination. In the Wu Xu imperial examination list, Prince Jia Kai was ranked first, Wang Maong, a Shilang (a low-ranking official), second, and Yan Tianxuan third. The emperor issued an edict: "Prince Jia Kai's exam results were indeed first according to the examiners' evaluation, but I do not wish for him to be crowned champion of the empire; therefore, the second-place candidate shall be placed at the top." A "suoting" candidate becoming the champion of the palace examination began with Wang Maong.

13 帝女号公主，壻为驸马都尉，近亲号郡主、县主，而壻俗呼郡马、县马，甚无义理。近世宗女既多，宗正立官媒数十人掌议婚，初不限阀阅。富家多赂宗室求婚，苟求一官，以庇门户，后相引为亲。京师富人如大桶张家，至有三十馀县主。

The daughters of the emperor were titled "gongzhu" (princesses), and their husbands held the title Fu Ma Du Wei (Marquises by Marriage). Close relatives were called Jun Zhu or Xian Zhu (Princesses of a lower rank), and their husbands were commonly referred to as Jun Ma or Xian Ma. This was an expression with no basis in reason or propriety. In recent times, as the number of daughters from the imperial clan increased, the Zongzheng office established dozens of official matchmakers to oversee marriage arrangements, and initially there were no restrictions based on family status or lineage. Wealthy families often bribed members of the imperial clan to seek marriages, merely hoping to obtain an official title in order to protect their family's status. Later, they would cite these connections as familial ties. Wealthy families in the capital, such as the Zhang family of Datong, had even married into dozens—over thirty—Xian Zhu (county princesses).

14 宣和殿，燕殿也，中贵人官高者皆直宣和殿。始置学士命蔡攸，置直学士命蔡翛、蔡鯈，置待制命蔡绦，后又置大学士命蔡攸，自盛章、王革、高佑皆相继为学士，班秩比延康殿学士为加优。凡外除则换延康，盖宣和职亲地近，非他比。己亥岁改保和殿。

The Xuanhe Dian was a banquet hall; high-ranking palace attendants were all assigned to serve directly in the Xuanhe Dian. The position of Xueshi (Academician) was first bestowed upon Cai You; the post of Zhixueshi (Direct Academician) was given to Cai Xiao and Cai Tiao; the title of Daizhao (Attendant Scholar) was conferred on Cai Tao. Later, the rank of Daxueshi (Grand Academician) was also established for Cai You. Subsequently, Sheng Zhang, Wang Ge, Gao You, and others successively held the position of Xueshi; their ranks were considered more prestigious than those of the Yan Kang Dian Xueshi. Whenever an official was reassigned to a post outside the palace, they would be transferred to Yankang Dian instead. This was because the posts at Xuanhe were considered closer in proximity and more intimate in status, unmatched by any others. In the Geng Hai year, it was changed to Baohedian (Hall of Harmony Preservation).

15 本朝五等之爵，自公、侯、伯、子、男，皆带本郡县开国，至封国公者则称某国公。初封小国，次移大国，以为恩数。亦有久不徙封者。文彦博初封潞国公，三十年不徙封。王安石初封舒国公，后徙荆国，既死，追封舒王，凡二国。蔡京初封嘉国，徙卫国、楚国、鲁国，凡四国，复加陈、鲁二国，公辞不拜。何执中初封荣国公，五年不徙封，薨于位，追封清源郡王，此仅事也。元佑初，司马光封温国公，议者以其刚厉，宜济之以温，东坡行麻词，亦云「封国于温，用旌直德」。崇宁初，曾布自相府以贿贬授廉州司户参军，议者以其贪墨，故箴之以廉，执笔者果有意乎？

In our dynasty, there are five ranks of noble titles: gong (duke), hou (marquis), bo (viscount), zi (ruler of a fief), and nan (baron). All these titles were associated with the names of their respective commanderies or counties as founding territories. Those who received the title of guogong (prince of a state) were addressed by the name of their state, such as "a certain Guo Gong." Initially, they were granted titles for small states; later, they were moved to larger ones, as a sign of increasing favor and reward. There were also those who remained in their original fiefs without being transferred for a long time. Wen Yanbo was initially granted the title of Luxi Guogong, and held this fief unchanged for thirty years. Wang Anshi was initially granted the title of Shu Guogong, later transferred to Jing Guogong. After his death, he was posthumously honored as Shu Wang; thus, he held titles for two states in total. Cai Jing was initially granted the title of Jia Guogong, then transferred to Wei Guo, Chu Guo, and Lu Guo—thus holding titles for four states. Later, he was further honored with the additional titles of Chen Guo and Lu Guo; however, as a duke, he declined these new appointments and did not accept them. He Zhong was initially granted the title Rong Guogong and held it for five years without being transferred. He died in office and was posthumously honored as Qingyuan Junwang; this was an exceptional case. In the early Yuanyou era, Sima Guang was granted the title of Wenguo Gong. Some commentators believed that because he was firm and austere, it was appropriate to bestow upon him a fief named "Wen" (meaning "gentle") as a complement. Su Shi, when drafting the official decree, also wrote: "The fief is given in Wen, to honor his upright virtue." In the early Chongning era, Zeng Bu was demoted from his post as chancellor and assigned to serve as a Shihu Cenjun (a minor official) in Lianzhou due to corruption. Commentators considered him greedy and corrupt, so they admonished him with the name "Lian" (meaning "integrity"). Did those who drafted this appointment indeed have an intentional message?

16 自元符、绍圣以前，大臣罕有除在京宫观者。两府召还为宫使侍读，甚稀阔。从官左迁，重者外移，轻者易职事。时有八座改枢密承旨、独座改工部侍郎，皆不美也。王震自吏部尚书移知开封府，又除枢密都承旨，王尝语先公曰：「震所谓齐一变至于鲁，鲁一变复至于齐者也。」政和间，近臣罢执政官，即授提举在京宫观，既体貌之，而名实相副。以罪去者，固自有法。

Before the Yuanfu and Shaosheng eras, high-ranking ministers rarely received appointments to palace temples or religious offices within the capital. It was very rare for officials from the two councils (the Secretariat and the Council of State) to be recalled to serve as palace attendants or imperial tutors. When lower-ranking officials were demoted, those with more serious offenses were transferred outside the capital; those with lesser faults had their duties changed instead. At that time, there was an instance where officials of the Eight Ministers were transferred to serve as Directors of Imperial Military Council Orders (Shumishi Chengzhi), and a single minister was reassigned to become a Minister of Works (Gongbu Shilang); these appointments were all considered unfavorable. Wang Zhen was transferred from Minister of Personnel to serve as Magistrate of Kaifeng Fu, and later appointed as Director of the Shumishi Du Chengzhi. Wang once told Xian gong: "Zhen is exactly what is meant by 'Qi changed slightly becomes Lu, and Lu changed slightly returns to Qi.'" During the Zhenghe era, when high-ranking courtiers were dismissed from their executive posts, they were immediately appointed as supervisors of palace temples and religious offices in the capital. This arrangement both showed respect for them and ensured that their titles matched their actual status. Those who left office due to crimes, of course, had their own legal consequences.

Only after returning home did he grieve for his deceased son. The imperial court was well aware of this. In between, during the Yuanyou era when policies were decided behind the curtain and leniency was emphasized, a special office for hearing appeals (Suli Suo) was established to clear the names of those who had been censured or punished in previous reigns. In the Gengchen year when a new emperor ascended the throne, Shu Dan finally regained an official post in the military. Coincidentally, at that time the Jing 蛮 (Jing Miao) people committed disturbances; he was therefore transferred to serve as commander of Nan Jun and appointed as Daidi Zhi (an honorary title). However, before he could assume his new duties, he died.

34 故事：宰相薨，驾幸浇奠，褰帷视尸，则所陈尚方金器尽赐其家，不举帷则收去。宰相吴充，元丰间薨于私第，上幸焉，夫人李氏徒跣下堂，叩头曰：「吴充贫，二子官六品，乞依两制例持丧，仍支俸。」诏许之。然仓卒白事，不及褰帷。驾兴，诸司敛器皿而去，计其所直，与二子特支俸颇相当，因谓官物有定分，不可妄得如此。

According to tradition, when a prime minister passed away, the emperor would personally visit to pour libations and pay respects. If he drew aside the curtain to view the body, all the golden ritual vessels presented by the imperial workshop (Shangfang) that had been displayed would be bestowed upon the family; if he did not lift the curtain, they were taken back. The prime minister Wu Chong died at his private residence during the Yuangong era. The emperor visited in person, and Lady Li, Wu's wife, went barefoot down to the hall and kowtowed, saying: "Wu Chong was poor; my two sons hold sixth-rank official positions. I beg Your Majesty to allow them to observe mourning according to the precedent for officials of both systems (Neishi and Waiwu), and also request that their salaries continue during this period." The emperor issued an edict granting her request. However, due to the sudden and hurried nature of the mourning arrangements, there was no time to lift the curtain [before the emperor left]. When the imperial procession departed, the various offices collected the ritual vessels and took them away. Calculating their value, it was found to be roughly equivalent to the special stipends granted to Wu Chong's two sons. Thus, people said that official property had its fixed allocation, and one could not obtain such things in this manner without justification.

A scholar from the capital region named Wang Tingli once served as a retainer for a border general, earned military merit, and was appointed as a military officer. He then went to court to argue that his father and grandfather had been civilian officials (wenchen), and since he himself had previously taken the imperial examination for the jinshi degree, he requested to be transferred into a civilian official post. Those in power had some who supported his request, and it eventually reached the emperor's attention. Wang Mengni thought to himself that after decades of hard work as a military officer, he had only just been promoted to the rank of shi chen (an official in charge of certain duties). If changing to a civilian post would allow him to be temporarily assigned to local county or prefectural positions, he was overjoyed. When the official notice arrived, he was appointed as an acting assistant teacher in Shizhou, but this position did not count toward his official selection rank, and he never held any administrative duties for life. Generally speaking, how can titles and official ranks be obtained through calculation or scheming?

36 先公素贫，元丰间，久于右史，奉亲甘旨不足，求外补。神考知之，将册贵妃，故事，两制奉册，执政读册，乃躐用先公为奉册官，门下侍郎章敦为读册官。中贵冯宗道密谓公言：「上知公贫，此盛礼也，必有厚赐。」既事，检会无册妃支赐例，止赐酒食而已。

My late father had always been poor. During the Yuangong era, after holding the post of Right Historiographer for a long time, he found it insufficient to provide his parents with nourishing food and delicacies, so he requested an appointment in an outlying region. Emperor Shenzong learned of this. When he was about to bestow the title of Noble Consort, by tradition, officials from both the Neishi and Waiwu systems would present the imperial edict (ce), while a senior minister would read it aloud. The emperor bypassed protocol and directly appointed my late father as the official in charge of presenting the edict, and Menxia Shilang Zhang Dun as the one to read it. A powerful eunuch named Feng Zongdao secretly told my late father: "The emperor knows you are poor. This is a grand ceremony, so there will certainly be generous gifts." In recent years, with the emperor's sons multiplying, there were frequent celebrations in the palace. On such occasions, the court often bestowed thousands of bolts of silk and taels of silver to high-ranking ministers as gifts. Although the Chief Minister held a position of great honor and prestige among court officials, even during the three occasions in the Xinsi, Yiyou, and Jichou years, there were times when he was not included in the gift distribution. Only He Zhong, due to his old favor from the imperial prince's household, rose from a subordinate official (Chengxia) to prime minister. From beginning to end, he never left office. Without counting other gifts and rewards, with 67皇子 and 帝姬 (imperial princesses), not one missed receiving the 包子 gift; thus, his family wealth surpassed that of all other officials. During the Shaosheng era, Cai Yuandu recommended him, and thus he was granted an audience with the emperor. Yuan Yingzhong had hunched shoulders, a short upper body and an unattractive lower half. He also had a broad forehead and pointed chin, with many dark moles on his face; from afar he looked as if ink had been sprinkled over it. His voice was hoarse and carried a Wu dialect accent. Emperor Zhezong, upon seeing him, repeatedly remarked that he was "extremely unattractive." Yuan Yingzhong, startled and unable to present his case, withdrew in embarrassment. Wang Jiong, a court official, was handsome and possessed both talent and literary ability. The accusation spread to the music bureau; today, in the song "Liu Mao," which mentions "The Handsome Young Man of the Wang Family" ("Qi Jun Wangjia Lang"), it refers to him. During the Yuangong era, Cai Chizheng recommended him as suitable for a supervisory post. Emperor Shenzong suddenly remarked: "Is this the 'Handsome Young Man of the Wang Family'?" 40 近制：中外库务、刑狱官、监司、守令、学官，假日许见客及出谒，在京台谏、侍从官以上，假日许受谒，不许出谒，谓之「谒禁」。士大夫以造请为勤，每遇休沐日，赍刺自旦至暮，遍走贵人门下。京局多私居，远近不一，极日力只能至数十处，往往计会阍者纳名刺上见客簿，未敢必见也。阍者得之，或弃去，或遗忘上簿。欲人相逢迎权要之门，则求赂，若稍不俯仰，便能窘人。兴国贾公衮自京师归，馀问物价贵贱，贾曰：「百物踊贵，只一味士大夫贱。」盖指奔竞者。尝闻蔡元长因阅门下见客簿，有一朝士，每日皆第一名到，如此累月。元长异之，召与语，可听，遂荐用至大官。太医学颜天选第三人及第，欲谒元长，未得见，乃随职事官入道史院。元长方对客，将命者觉其非本局官，揖退之，天选不肯出，吏稍掖之，天选抱柱而呼曰：「颜天选见太师！」与吏相持，帻忽堕地，元长命引至前，语之曰：「公少年高科，乃不自爱惜！道史与国史同例，奈何阑入此耶！」天选整帻而出，吏执送开封府鞫罪，特旨除名，送宿州编管，自此士风稍革。

According to recent regulations: officials in charge of treasury affairs, judicial and penal offices, supervisory posts, prefects and magistrates, as well as educational officers, are allowed on holidays to receive guests or pay visits. In the capital, officials at the level of censors, remonstrators, attendants, and higher may accept visitors during holidays but are not permitted to make outgoing calls; this restriction is known as "Yejin" (the prohibition on visiting). Scholar-officials regarded making visits as a sign of diligence. On every day off, they would carry visiting cards from dawn until dusk, going door to door across the residences of high-ranking officials. Many official residences in the capital were privately located, scattered at varying distances. Even with all their effort, officials could only visit dozens of places in a day. Often, they had to arrange through gatekeepers to have their visiting cards recorded in the guest register, yet there was no guarantee that an audience would be granted. The gatekeepers, upon receiving them, might discard the visiting cards or forget to record them in the register. If someone wished to meet at the gates of powerful officials, they had to offer bribes; if one showed even slight reluctance to comply, the gatekeepers could easily put them in an awkward or humiliating position. Jia Gonggun of Xingguo, returning from the capital, was asked by me about the prices of goods. Jia replied: "All things have become greatly expensive; only one thing has become cheap—the scholar-officials." He meant those who rushed about vying for favor and positions. I once heard that Cai Yuanzhang, while reviewing the guest register of the Menxia Department, found one court official who was always listed as the first visitor each day for many consecutive months. Yuanzhang found this unusual and summoned him for a conversation, during which he was impressed by the man's ability to speak coherently. He then recommended and promoted him to an important official post. He therefore followed his official duties into Daoshiyuan (the Office of Historical Records). Cai Yuanzhang was just entertaining guests when the attendant realized that Yan Tianxuan was not an official from this bureau and gestured for him to leave. Yan Tianxuan refused to exit, so a clerk gently pushed him aside. In response, Yan Tianxuan clung to a pillar and shouted: "Yan Tianxuan requests an audience with the Taishi!" The confrontation between Yan Tianxuan and the clerk continued, when suddenly his official headpiece fell to the ground. Cai Yuanzhang then ordered him to be brought forward and said to him: "You are a young man of high achievement, yet you do not respect yourself!" The Daoshiyuan is on equal footing with the Guoshiyuan (Imperial History Office); how could you have dared to enter here without authorization!" Yan Tianxuan straightened his headpiece and left. The clerk then took him to Kaifeng Fu for interrogation and punishment. By special imperial decree, he was stripped of his official status and sent to Suzhou for registration and supervision. From this incident onward, the conduct of scholar-officials gradually improved.

During the reign of the ancestors, jinshi candidates taking the palace examination were required to personally write out their responses to three topics: poetry, fu (a literary form), and a prose essay. In the third year of Xining, the palace examination used the "ce" (policy essay) format and required a copy to be made. This was done in accordance with the method of anonymous grading (huming), to demonstrate utmost impartiality, and to prevent minor forms of corruption from arising. In recent years, the sons and relatives of high-ranking ministers have often occupied examination ranks. Zhang Dun, when serving as prime minister, had his son Zhi and grandson Dian ranked first in the jinshi examination. Xu Jiang, while holding the post of Menxia Shilang (Minister of the Gate of Supreme Harmony), had his son Fen ranked first. Xue Ang, during his tenure as Shangshu Zuoceng (Left Vice Minister of the Ministry of Works), had his son Shangyou ranked first. Zheng Juzhong, when serving as prime minister, also had his son Nianye ranked first in the examination. Some may suspect that the method of anonymous grading is somewhat lax; this is not the case. During the palace examination, policy essays were required to address recent court affairs; candidates from distant regions could not possibly be familiar with these matters. However, the sons of high-ranking ministers had long been accustomed to hearing discussions on such topics and thus consistently succeeded in their exams.

Yan Yuanxian had always been upright and sincere since childhood, while Cai Jingfan was the most pliant and fawning. Whenever the crown prince passed through a threshold or gatepost, Cai would kneel on the ground and allow the prince to step on his back in order to ascend. After ascending the throne, Emperor Renzong favored Yan Yuanxian, who eventually rose to become prime minister. Cai Jingfan, however, was never greatly employed. By virtue of old favor, he often held local governorships but did not abide by laws and regulations; on occasion, he was impeached and required to await imperial instructions. The emperor recognized his name and would always say: "He is an old retainer from my princely household; let him be transferred to another post." He served through four reigns and retired at the beginning of the Yuanfu era, by which time he was already eighty years old. The supervisory official recommended him, requesting that he be allowed to retire from retirement and resume a post at an imperial temple or shrine. The recommendation briefly stated: "Cai Mou is eighty years old and has received official stipends for seventy-five years." I believe that although people may attain fame and position in life, it is rare indeed to achieve such enduring longevity as this.

44 政和壬辰榜唱名，有饶州神童赴殿试中第，才十数岁，又侏儒，既释褐，卫士抱之，于幕上作傀儡戏，中贵人大笑。次日特奏名人唱第，皆引近殿陛，恣其所陈，有自诉病者，出尚药珍剂赐之。

During the political and cultural flourishing of the Zhenghe era, on the Renchen imperial examination list, there was a child prodigy from Raozhou who attended the palace examination and passed. He was only ten or so years old and also very short in stature. After receiving his official robe (i.e., passing the exam), he was carried by guards onto the stage where they performed puppet shows on a curtain for entertainment, which greatly amused the powerful eunuchs. The following day, during the special imperial examination for those granted official status by special decree (techong mingren), all candidates were brought close to the throne and allowed to present their petitions freely. Some petitioned on grounds of illness, and the emperor would then have rare medicinal remedies from the imperial pharmacy presented and bestowed upon them.

45 饶州杜神童释褐，父携之谢政府，才八九岁，客次中士大夫皆孩之，或戏云：「来学政事文字否？」答曰：「非也，待告相公，求一堂除差遣。」言者大惭。

When the child prodigy Du from Raozhou received his official robe, his father brought him to thank the government officials. The boy was only eight or nine years old; in the waiting hall, scholar-officials all treated him as a child, some jokingly asking: "Have you come to learn about state affairs and official documents?" He replied: "No. I have come to inform the minister, to request a minor official post." Those who heard this were greatly embarrassed.

46 元丰间，特奏名陛试，有老生七十许岁，于试卷内书云：「臣老矣，不能为文也，伏愿陛下万岁万万岁。」既闻，上嘉其诚，特给初品官，食俸终其身。

During the Yuangong era, in a special imperial examination (techong ming) for candidates granted official status by decree, there was an elderly examinee about seventy years old. On his exam paper he wrote: "I am now very old and unable to compose essays; I humbly pray that Your Majesty may live ten thousand years upon ten thousand years." Upon hearing this, the emperor praised his sincerity and specially granted him a first-rank official title, allowing him to receive a stipend for life.

47 禁中应奉者多避语忌。大观中，主文柄者专务奉上，于是程文有疑似之禁，虽无明文，犯必黜落，举子靡然成风。如「大哉尧之为君」、「君哉舜也」，皆以与灾字同音，并不用；「反者道之动」，易反为复，「九变而赏罚可言」，易变为更，此类不一。能文者执笔不敢下，憸夫善逢迎，往往在高第。政和初，言者论之，降诏宣谕：「虽暗于大体者，或以为忠，然爱君果在兹乎！」尝侍先公，闻说元丰时岁歉，流民过国门，闽人郑侠监新城门，图其状以谏。既不可上达，乃作边檄，夜传入禁中。适永乐失律，上常西顾，檄至无敢遏，方秉烛启封，见图画饥民饿殍无数，穷愁寒态不一，罔测何事，良久始知侠所上谏书也。翌日降旨，投侠广南。不识忌讳，又有如此者。

Those who served in the imperial palace often avoided using words that might be considered taboo or disrespectful. During the Daguan era, those in charge of the examination system focused entirely on flattery and pleasing the emperor. As a result, there were strict prohibitions against any content in examination essays that seemed even slightly questionable or potentially disrespectful. Although these restrictions had no official written regulations, anyone who violated them was inevitably disqualified. This practice gradually became a prevailing trend among examinees. For example, phrases such as "Great was Yao's reign as a ruler" ("Da zai Yao zhi wei jun") and "Sovereign indeed was Shun" ("Jun zai Shun ye") were avoided because they sounded similar to the word for disaster (zai). Similarly, the phrase "Fan zhe dao zhi dong" ("Opposition is the movement of the Dao") had its "fan" changed to "fu," and in "Jiu bian er shangfa ke yan" ("After nine changes, rewards and punishments can be discussed"), the word "bian" was altered to "geng." There were numerous such examples. Those who were talented in writing hesitated to put pen to paper, fearing violations of these rules; while sycophants skilled at flattery and conformity often ranked highest. In the early Zhenghe era, officials raised criticism about this practice. The emperor issued an imperial edict to address it: "Although some who lack a broad understanding may regard such actions as loyalty, can true devotion to the sovereign really lie in this?" I once served under my late father and heard him speak of the Yuangong era when there was a poor harvest, and displaced people passed through the capital's gates. A native of Min, Zheng Xia, who was in charge of monitoring Xin Cheng Gate, drew pictures depicting their plight to remonstrate with the emperor. When he could not get his petition delivered directly, Zheng Xia composed a military dispatch and sent it secretly into the palace at night. Coincidentally, at the time there had been a military defeat in Yongle, and the emperor was often looking westward with concern. When the dispatch arrived, no one dared to intercept it; he immediately lit a candle and opened the seal. Inside were drawings of countless starving civilians and corpses, each depicting different expressions of poverty and despair. For some time, he could not fathom what the content might be, but after careful examination, he finally realized that this was Zheng Xia's memorial of remonstration. The next day, an imperial decree was issued, exiling Zheng Xia to Guangnan. There were those who did not know how to avoid taboos and speak cautiously—Zheng Xia was one such example.

48 姚佑元符初为杭州学教授，堂试诸生，《易》题出《乾为金坤亦为金何也》。先是，福建书籍刊板舛错，「坤为釜」遗二点，故姚误读作金。诸生疑之，因上请，姚复为臆说，而诸生或以诚告，姚取官本视之，果「釜」也，大惭，曰：「佑买著福建本！」升堂自罚一直，其不护短如此。

Yao You, in the early Yuanfu era, served as a professor at Hangzhou Xue (the local academy). During an examination for students, he set a question on the I Ching (Book of Changes) titled: "Why is Qian associated with metal and Kun also associated with metal?" Previously, the printed editions of books in Fujian had errors in their woodblock carvings; the character for "Kun is a cauldron" ("坤为釜") was missing two dots, so Yao mistakenly read it as meaning "metal." The students were puzzled and thus submitted a request for clarification. Yao You offered an arbitrary explanation, but some of the students honestly informed him of the error. Yao then retrieved the official edition to check it himself, and indeed, it read "釜" (cauldron), not "金" (metal). Deeply embarrassed, he said: "Yao has bought a Fujian edition!" He ascended the hall and personally punished himself by deducting one zhi (a unit of punishment or compensation), demonstrating such integrity in not covering up his mistake.

49 先公尝言，昔在修撰经义局，与诸子聚首，介甫见举烛因言：「佛书有日月灯光明佛，灯光岂足以配日月？」吉甫曰：「日煜昼，月煜夜，灯煜昼夜，日月所不及，其用无差别。」介甫大以为然。吉甫所言中理，历历可记类如此。

My late father once said that in the past, when he was working at the Jingyi Ju (Bureau for Editing Classical Texts), and gathered with his colleagues, Jiefu saw someone holding up a candle and remarked: "In Buddhist scriptures there is a Buddha called Ruyue Dengguang Ming Fo (the Buddha of Solar and Lunar Lamp Light). How can lamp light possibly be compared to the sun and moon?" Jifu replied: "The sun illuminates the day, the moon illuminates the night, but a lamp illuminates both day and night—something the sun and moon cannot achieve. In terms of their function, there is no difference." Jiefu was greatly convinced by this argument. What Jifu said was well-reasoned and precise, and there were many similar instances worth recording in detail.

50 杜甫诗虽屡经校正，然有从来舛谬相袭者，后人钦其名，更不究义理，如「己公茅屋」诗一联云：「江莲摇白羽，天棘梦青丝。」二语是何情理？「摇」对「梦」，轻重不称，读者未闻商搉，亦好古之癖也。余窃谓当作「蔓青丝」，此类亦多，未可徧举。

Du Fu's poetry, although repeatedly proofread and corrected, still contains errors that have been passed down through generations. Later scholars, out of reverence for his name, no longer carefully examine the meaning or logic; for example, in one couplet from the poem "Jigong Mao Wu" ("The Thatched Cottage of Jigong"), it reads: "River lotuses sway like white feathers, Heavenly thorns dream of green threads." What kind of logic or imagery do these two lines convey? The word "yao" (sway) is paired with "meng" (dream), yet their weight and tone are mismatched. Readers have never heard of anyone questioning or debating this, which also reflects a kind of antiquarian obsession. I secretly believe it should be "man qing si" (winding green threads). There are many similar cases, too numerous to list exhaustively.

51 东坡自云：尝梦至帝所，见侍女月娥仙，为作裙带诗，其词曰：「百迭漪漪水皱，六铢纚纚云轻。植立广寒深殿，风来环佩微声。」

Dongpo once said: I once dreamed of arriving at the imperial palace, where I saw a lady-in-waiting named Yue'e Xian. For her, I composed a poem about a sash, which read: "A hundred folds ripple like wrinkled water; six zhu [a unit] of flowing clouds are light." She stood in the vast, cold hall of Guanghan, where even the wind brought only a faint sound from her jade pendants.

52 子瞻曾为先公言：「书传间出迭字，皆作二小画于其下。乐府有《瑟二调歌》，平时读作『瑟瑟』，后到海南，见一黥卒，自云元系教坊瑟二部头，方知当作『瑟二』，非『瑟瑟』也。」子瞻好学，弥老不衰，类皆如此。余尝访教坊瑟二事，云每色以二人，如笛二、筝二，总谓之「色二」，不作「瑟」字，不知果如何。

Zizhan once told my late father: "In ancient texts and records, whenever the character 'die' appears, it is usually written with two small strokes beneath it." The Yuefu anthology includes a song titled "Se Er Diao Ge" ("Song of the Two Se Tunes"). Normally, it is read as "sesi," but later when I arrived in Hainan, I met an enslaved soldier who claimed he had originally been the leader of the two Se sections of the Jiaofang (Imperial Music Bureau). Only then did I realize that it should be read as "Se Er" ("Two Se"), not "Sesi."" Zizhan was deeply devoted to learning, and his enthusiasm for study never waned with age; such instances were common in his life. I once inquired about the matter of Jiaofang's "Se Er," and was told that for each type of instrument, two people were assigned; for example, "Di Er" (two flutes), "Zheng Er" (two zithers). Collectively they were called "Se Er," not written with the character "瑟." I do not know whether this is indeed accurate.

53 姓氏之学，近世不复讲，以名讳改者，多失其旨。钱鏐据吴越，改刘为金，姓谱自有金氏，后世不知其源者，金与刘通婚姻。本朝改殷为商或汤，改敬为文或苟，一姓分为二，后世可通婚姻乎？又不协旧音，如「文苟」为敬，太觉踈脱，盖一时任其自改，所以失之。近制改匡为康，天为轩，以声音相近为例，且从上令也。政和间有营卒天安，差隶陈彦以闻，乃诏改之。勘会到天安父尚在，未闻此姓所出，岂异种乎？氏族之学久废，小人或妄改，或相传舛缪至于此，亦不可不知也。

The study of surnames and family names has been neglected in recent times, and many have lost their original meanings due to changes made out of respect for naming taboos. Qian Liu, who ruled Wu Yue, changed the surname Liu to Jin. However, there had already been a Jin clan in the genealogical records; later generations, unaware of its origin, allowed intermarriage between those surnamed Jin and Liu. In our dynasty, the surname Yin was changed to Shang or Tang, and Jing was altered to Wen or Gou. One surname thus split into two—can later generations intermarry between them? Moreover, these changes do not align with the old pronunciations; for example, "Wen Gou" as a substitute for Jing feels too abrupt and disconnected. It seems that at the time people were allowed to change their surnames freely, which is why such errors occurred. The recent regulation changed Kuang to Kang and Tian to Xuan, using similar sounds as a basis for the change, and this was also in compliance with imperial orders. During the Zhenghe era, there was a soldier named Tian An, who was reported by Chen Yan for assignment. The emperor then issued an edict ordering his surname to be changed. It was verified that Tian An's father was still alive, and there had been no record of the origin of this surname; could it be from an unusual or foreign lineage? The study of family lineages has long been neglected, and common people may arbitrarily alter surnames or pass down erroneous information, leading to such confusion. This is something that should not be overlooked.

54 施结大夫，更鄱阳、兴国、庐陵郡守，性好蓄古今人押字。押字自唐以来方有之，盖亦署名之类，但草书不甚谨，故或谓之草字。韦陟署名五朵云，此押字所起也，其后不复与名相类，而阴阳家又生吉凶之论。施所蓄甚多，如唐末藩镇所署，极有奇怪者，跋扈之徒，事事放恣。本朝前辈虽官尊，尤谨小，可以此观人度量。施尽以刻石，每移徙，用数人负之而行，其癖如此。光州马大夫知彭州还乡，凡私居文书，纸尾皆署「使」字押号。溱州牧孙伟，赏言见太师府揭示，承令寺监官两员以上许见宰相，纸尾署「官」字，公相押号。

Shi Jie Dafu, having served successively as the governor of Polyang, Xingguo, and Luling commanderies, had a fondness for collecting signature seals from ancient and modern figures. Signature seals first appeared since the Tang dynasty; they are also a kind of name inscription. However, because cursive script was not written with great care, some referred to them as "cao zi" (i.e., cursive characters). Wei Zhi signed his name as "Wuduo Yun," from which the practice of signature seals originated. Afterwards, these signatures no longer closely resembled one's actual name, and diviners further introduced theories about their auspiciousness or inauspiciousness. Shi Jie collected a great many of these, including signatures from the warlords of the late Tang dynasty, some of which were extremely strange. These arrogant and lawless individuals acted with total disregard for restraint in all matters. The predecessors of our dynasty, though holding high official positions, were especially cautious about even minor details; one could judge a person's character and breadth of mind through such conduct. Shi Jie had all his collections carved into stone, and whenever he moved, it required several people to carry them along. His obsession was this intense. When the Maguozhou Dafu returned to his hometown after serving as governor of Pengzhou, all private documents and writings he kept at home had a signature seal marked with the character "Shi" at the bottom of each sheet. Sun Wei, the governor of Qin Zhou, once saw a notice displayed at the Taishi Fu (Grand Chancellor's Office), which stated that if two or more officials from the Chengling Si Jian Guan (Imperial Command Supervisory Officials) wished to meet the prime minister, they were permitted. At the bottom of such documents, it was required to write the character "Guan" and have the official seal affixed by the chancellor.

55 吴处厚善属辞，知汉阳军，每谓鹦鹉洲沔、鄂佳处，欲赋诗未就。一日视事，纲吏来告覆舟，吴问所在，吏曰：「在鸬鹚堰。」吴拊案连唱大奇，徐曰：「吾一年为鹦鹉洲寻一对未得，天庇汝也。」因得末减。王梅运勾，骨立有风味，朋从目之为风流骸骨。崇宁癸未，馀在金陵府集，见官妓中有极瘦者，府尹朱世英语馀曰：「亦识生色髑髅否？」馀欣然为王得对。

Wu Chuhou was skilled in composing elegant prose and served as the commander of Hanyang Jun. He often remarked on how beautiful the areas of Yingwu Zhou, Mian, and E were, and had long wished to compose a poem about them but had not yet completed it. One day while attending official business, a clerk came to report that a ship had capsized. Wu asked where it happened, and the clerk replied: "It occurred at Luzuiyan." Wu struck the desk in succession, exclaiming with great excitement. He then slowly said: "For a whole year I have been searching for a couplet to describe Yingwu Zhou without success; Heaven has now provided it for you." He thus received a reduced punishment. Wang Mei Yun gou was gaunt yet full of charm, and his companions jokingly referred to him as "a fashionable skeleton." In the Chongning gengwu year, I was at the Jinling Fu Ji (Prefectural Office in Jinling), where I saw an official courtesan who was extremely thin. The governor Zhu Shiying said to me: "Do you know what a 'shengse skull' is?" I happily provided Wang with the couplet he sought.

56 元丰间，御史中丞舒亶以罪除名勒停，及僦客舟东归，时有诏召僧慈本住慧林，许驰驿，轻薄者以「中丞赁航船出京，和尚乘递马赴阙」为对，以见异事。

During the Yuangong era, the Vice Minister of Censorate Shu Dan was stripped of his title and dismissed from office due to a crime. When he rented a passenger boat to return eastward, there happened to be an imperial edict summoning the monk Ciben to reside at Huilin Temple, allowing him to travel by official relay horse. The frivolous people made a couplet: "The vice censor rents a ferryboat to leave the capital; the monk rides a postal horse to go to court," using it to highlight this unusual contrast.

57 大观间，翰苑进春帖子，有一学士撰词云：「神只祖考安乐之，草木鸟兽裕如也。」以鸟兽对祖考，非所宜，竟以是得罪。

During the Daguan era, when the Hanyuan (Hall of Literary Men) presented spring couplets, one scholar-official composed a verse that read: "The gods and ancestors enjoy peace and happiness; grasses, trees, birds, and beasts flourish abundantly." To pair "birds and beasts" with "ancestors" was inappropriate, and he was ultimately punished for this.

58 蔡持正自左揆责知安州，尝作《安陆十诗》，吴处厚捃摭笺注，蔡坐此贬新州。其诗有云：「睡起莞然成独笑，数声渔笛在沧浪。」处厚注云：「未知蔡确此时独笑何事。」先公帅广，崇宁元年正月游蒲涧，因越俗也。见游人簪凤尾花，作口号，中一联云：「孤臣正泣龙须草，游子空簪凤尾花。」盖以被遇先朝，自伤流落。后监司互论，乃指此句以为罪，其诬注云：「契勘正月十二日，哲宗皇帝已大祥，岂是孤臣正泣之时！」鞫狱竟无他意，谗口可畏如此。

Cai Chizheng, demoted from his post as Left Chancellor to serve as governor of Anzhou, once composed ten poems titled "Anlu Shi Shi." Wu Chuhou collected and annotated these poems, and Cai was consequently exiled to Xin Zhou for this. One of his poems reads: "Awakening from sleep, I smile alone; a few notes of the fisherman's flute echo over Canglang." Chuhou annotated it by saying: "It is unknown what Pekuo was laughing about at this moment alone." My late father, when serving as commander of Guangdong, visited Pu Jian in the first month of Chongning Yuan Nian, following a local custom. He saw travelers wearing phoenix-tail flowers and composed an impromptu poem. One couplet in it read: "A lonely minister weeps over the dragon's beard grass, while a wandering son vainly adorns himself with phoenix-tail blossoms." This was because he had been favored by the previous dynasty and lamented his own exile and misfortune. Later, when the supervisory officials disputed with each other, they cited this line as a charge against him. Their false accusation read: "Upon investigation, on the twelfth day of the first month, Emperor Zhezong had already completed the major mourning period (daxiang). How could it be the time for a 'lonely minister' to weep?" The investigation into the case ultimately found no other intent; yet malicious gossip was so fearsome.

59 宣和初，荆州掾见僧房有异花不知名，僧云：「花气酷烈不可近。」掾因题诗云：「山花红与绿，日暮颜色足。无名我不识，有毒君莫触。」后有人谮掾于苏漕，指此诗曰：「湖南漕宪俱衣绯，馀皆衣绿，无衣紫者。苏漕最老，又独无出身，数发摘官吏，故掾托意山花，实以嘲漕。」苏大怒，竟捃摭掾。

In the early Xuanhe era, a clerk in Jingzhou saw an unusual flower with an unknown name growing in a monk's quarters. The monk said: "The fragrance of this flower is extremely strong and unbearable to be near." The clerk thus composed a poem, which read: "Mountain flowers red and green, at dusk their colors are most vivid. Nameless, I do not recognize; if it is toxic, you must not touch." Later, someone slandered the clerk to Su Cao (the Transport Commissioner of Hunan), pointing to this poem and saying: "In Hunan, both the commissioner of transport and the censors wear crimson robes; others all wear green, but no one wears purple." Su Cao was the eldest and also uniquely lacked official credentials, yet he frequently exposed and censured officials. Therefore, the clerk used the imagery of mountain flowers to subtly mock Su Cao." Su Cao became extremely angry and ultimately persecuted the clerk by collecting trivial charges against him.

60 王介甫居金陵，作《谢公墩》诗云：「我名公字偶相同，我屋公墩在眼中，公去我来墩属我，不应墩姓尚随公。」盖晋谢安故地也，谢字安石，介甫名安石。

Wang Jiefu, while residing in Jinling, composed a poem titled "Xie Gong Dun," which read: "My name and his surname happen to be the same; my house and his mound are visible before me. He has gone and I have come—this mound now belongs to me. The mound's name should not still follow him." This was the former site of Xie An from the Jin dynasty; his courtesy name was Ans Shi, and Jiefu's given name was also Anshi.

61 苏子瞻责黄州，居州之东坡，作雪堂，自号「东坡居士」，后人遂目子瞻为东坡，其地今属佛庙。子瞻元佑中知杭州，筑大堤西湖上，人呼为苏公堤，属吏刻石榜名。世俗以富贵相高，以堤音低，颇为语忌。未几，子瞻迁责。时孟氏作后，京师衣饰画作双蝉，目为孟家蝉，识者谓蝉有禅意，久之后竟废。

Su Zizhan, when exiled to Huangzhou, lived in the eastern slope of the city and built a hall called Xuetang. He gave himself the sobriquet "Dongpo Jushi." Later generations thus referred to Zizhan as Dongpo. The site is now part of a Buddhist temple. Zizhan served as the governor of Hangzhou during Yuanyou, and constructed a large dike along West Lake. The people called it "Sugong Di" (Su Gong's Dike), and local officials carved stone plaques to mark its name. The common people often compare wealth and status, and since the word "di" (dike) sounds like "di" (low), they found it somewhat inauspicious to use. Not long after, Zizhan was transferred and exiled again. At that time, after the Meng family came to power, in the capital people's clothing and decorations featured images of double cicadas, which were called "Mengjia Chans." Those who understood symbolism said that cicadas carried a meaning related to Chan (Buddhism), and eventually this fashion was abandoned.

62 元丰间诏僧慈本住慧林禅院，召见赐茶，以为荣遇。先公侍上，见宣谕慈本云：「京师繁盛，细民逐末，朕要卿来，劝人作善。」别无他语。建中靖国元年，召诣禁中，赐十字师号及御制《僧惟白续灯录叙》。释徒尤以为盛事。其后赐僧楷四字禅师号，楷固不受以钓名，推避之际颇不恭，朝廷正其罪，投之远方，无他异，术穷情露，教遂不振。又狂逆不道，伐冢诱略，多出浮屠中，宣和初乃译正其教，改僧为德士，复姓氏，完发肤，正冠裳，尽革其故俗云。

During the Yuangong era, an imperial edict summoned the monk Ciben to reside in Huilin Chan Temple and granted him an audience with tea presented as a gift, which was considered a great honor. My late father served in attendance on the emperor, and witnessed the imperial instructions given to Ciben, which said: "The capital is prosperous, but common people chase trivial pursuits. I have summoned you here to encourage them to do good." There were no other words spoken. In the first year of Jianzhongjingguo, he was summoned to the imperial palace and bestowed with a ten-character title for monks as well as an imperial composition titled "Xu Deng Lu Xu" by Emperor Renzong. The Buddhist community especially regarded this as a momentous event. Later, the court bestowed a four-character Chan master title upon the monk Kai. However, Kai deliberately refused it in order to cultivate a reputation for humility; during his refusal, he behaved rather disrespectfully. The court then punished him for this offense and exiled him to a distant land. There was nothing extraordinary about this—his pretense collapsed as his true intentions were revealed—and thus the Buddhist teachings gradually declined in influence. Moreover, there were many cases of reckless and rebellious behavior—grave robbing, luring people into wrongdoing—that often originated from among the Buddhist monks. In the early Xuanhe period, the court officially reformed their religion, changing the term "seng" (monk) to "deshi," restoring family names, allowing them to grow hair and keep their skin intact, requiring proper hats and robes, and completely abolishing all of their old customs.

In the capital, when purchasing a concubine, each cost five thousand coins; a beautiful one could sell for thirty to fifty such units. Once the contract was finalized, parents and relatives would still demand additional payments, which were called "bian shou qian." In our dynasty, noble families seeking a son-in-law would select candidates during the examination years, choosing those who had passed the provincial exams. 1 广州市舶司旧制：帅臣漕使领提举市舶事，祖宗时谓之市舶使。福建路泉州，两浙路明州、杭州，皆傍海，亦有市舶司。崇宁初，三路各置提举市舶官，三方唯广最盛，官吏或侵渔，则商人就易处，故三方亦迭盛衰。朝廷尝并泉州舶船令就广，商人或不便之。

The old system of the Guangzhou Shiyi Shi (Office of Maritime Trade) was as follows: The military commander and canal commissioner would oversee the duties of the director of maritime trade. In the time of our ancestors, this official was called the "Shiyishi" (Director of Maritime Trade). Quanzhou in Fujian Lu, Mingzhou and Hangzhou in Liangzhe Lu, are all located near the sea and also have Shiyi Shi (Offices of Maritime Trade). In the beginning of Chongning era, each of the three circuits established an officer in charge of maritime trade. Among these three regions, Guangzhou was the most prosperous. However, if officials and clerks sometimes exploited or harassed them, merchants would move to more favorable places; thus, the prosperity and decline of the three regions alternated. The imperial court once consolidated the merchant ships from Quanzhou to report to Guangzhou, which was inconvenient for some merchants.

2 广州自小海至溽洲七百里，溽洲有望舶巡检司，谓之一望，稍北又有第二、第三望，过溽洲则沧溟矣。商船去时，至溽洲少需以诀，然后解去，谓之「放洋」。还至溽洲，则相庆贺，寨兵有酒肉之馈，并防护赴广州。既至，泊船市舶亭下，五洲巡检司差兵监视，谓之「编栏」。凡舶至，帅漕与市舶监官莅阅其货而征之，谓之「抽解」，以十分为率，真珠龙脑凡细色抽一分，瑇瑁苏木凡粗色抽三分，抽外官市各有差，然后商人得为己物。象牙重及三十斤并乳香，抽外尽官市，盖榷货也。商人有象牙稍大者，必截为三斤以下，规免官市。凡官市价微，又准他货与之，多折阅，故商人病之。舶至未经抽解，敢私取物货者，虽一毫皆没其馀货，科罪有差，故商人莫敢犯。

Guangzhou is seven hundred li from Xiaohai to Ru Zhou. At Ru Zhou there was a Xunjian Si (patrol office) for inspecting merchant ships, called "Yiwang." Slightly north of it were the Second and Third Wangs. Beyond Ru Zhou lay the vast ocean. When merchant ships departed, they would stop at Ru Zhou for a brief farewell before setting off; this was called "Fangyang." When returning and arriving at Ru Zhou, the merchants would celebrate together. The garrison soldiers would offer food and drink as gifts and also provide protection on their way to Guangzhou. Upon arrival, the ships would be moored under the Shiyi Ting (Maritime Trade Pavilion). The Wuzhou Xunjian Si (Patrol Office of Five Isles) would dispatch soldiers to supervise them; this was called "Bianlan." Whenever a merchant ship arrived, the military commander, canal commissioner, and maritime trade supervisors would inspect its cargo and collect duties; this was called "Choujie." The rate of taxation was based on ten parts: for fine goods such as pearls and camphor, one part was levied; for coarse goods like tortoiseshell and su mu (a type of wood), three parts were levied. In addition to the tax, official purchases varied according to different categories. Only after these procedures could merchants consider the remaining goods as their own property. For ivory weighing thirty jin or more and frankincense, after the initial levy, all remaining goods were purchased by the government; this was a form of state monopoly. Merchants who had larger pieces of ivory would necessarily cut them into portions weighing less than three jin in order to evade official purchase. The official purchase price was usually low, and merchants were often allowed to exchange other goods instead; this frequently resulted in losses for them. Therefore, merchants found this system burdensome. If a merchant ship arrived and had not yet undergone the Choujie (levy) process, anyone who dared to privately take goods would have all their remaining cargo confiscated, even if only a single hair's worth was taken. Punishments for such offenses varied in severity; therefore, no merchant dared to commit such an act.

3 广州市舶亭枕水有海山楼，正对五洲，其下谓之小海，中流方丈馀，舶船取其水，贮以过海，则不坏。逾此丈许取者并汲井水，皆不可贮，久则生虫，不知此何理也。舶船去以十一月、十二月，就北风，来以五月、六月，就南风。船方正若一木斛，非风不能动。其樯植定而帆侧挂，以一头就樯柱如门扇，帆席谓之「加突」，方言也。海中不唯使顺风，开岸就岸风皆可使，唯风逆则倒退尔，谓之使三面风，逆风尚可用矴石不行。广帅以五月祈风于丰隆神。

The Shiyi Ting (Maritime Pavilion) of Guangzhou is situated by the water with a Hai Shan Lou (Seashell Tower), directly facing Wuzhou. The area below it is called Xiaohai, where in midstream there are square stones left above water; merchant ships would take water from here and store it for crossing the sea, as this water would not spoil. Water taken beyond this point by even a few chi or drawn from well water was all unsuitable for storage; if kept for long, it would breed insects. No one knew the reason behind this phenomenon. Merchant ships departed in the eleventh and twelfth lunar months, taking advantage of the north wind, and arrived in the fifth and sixth lunar months, following the south wind. The ships were square and solid like a single wooden measuring vessel; without wind, they could not move. Their masts were fixed upright and the sails hung on the side, with one end attached to the mast like a door panel. The sailcloth was called "Jiatu," which is local dialect. At sea, one does not rely solely on following the wind; by adjusting course toward or away from the shore, ships can also make use of crosswinds. Only when facing a headwind would they move backward; this technique was called "using three-sided winds." Even against an adverse wind, it was still possible to maneuver using anchor stones, though progress would be difficult. The military commander of Guangzhou would pray for favorable winds to the Fenglong Shen (Fenglong Deity) in the fifth lunar month.

4 甲令：海舶大者数百人，小者百馀人，以巨商为纲首、副纲首、杂事，市舶司给朱记，许用笞治其徒，有死亡者籍其财。商人言船大人众则敢往，海外多盗贼，且掠非诣其国者，如诣占城，或失路误入真腊，则尽没其舶货，缚北人卖之，云：「尔本不来此间。」外国虽无商税，而诛求，谓之献送，不论货物多寡，一例责之，故不利小舶也。舶船深阔各数十丈，商人分占贮货，人得数尺许，下以贮物，夜卧其上。货多陶器，大小相套，无少隙地。海中不畏风涛，唯惧靠阁，谓之「凑浅」，则不复可脱。船忽发漏，既不可入治，令鬼奴持刀絮自外补之，鬼奴善游，入水不瞑。舟师识地理，夜则观星，昼则观日，阴晦观指南针，或以十丈绳钩，取海底泥嗅之，便知所至。海中无雨，凡有雨则近山矣。商人言舶船遇无风时，海水如鉴。舟人捕鱼，用大钩如臂，缚一鸡骛为饵，使大鱼吞之，随其行半日方困，稍近之，又半日，方可取，忽遇风，则弃。或取得大鱼不可食，剖腹求所吞小鱼可食，一腹不下数十枚，枚数十斤。海大鱼每随舶上下，凡投物无不啖。舟人病者忌死于舟中，往往气未绝便卷以重席，投水中，欲其遽沈，用数瓦罐贮水缚席间，才投入，群鱼并席吞去，竟不少沈。有锯鲨长百十丈，鼻骨如锯，遇舶船，横截断之如拉朽尔。舶行海中，忽远视枯木山积，舟师疑此处旧无山，则蛟龙也，乃断发取鱼鳞骨同焚，稍稍没水中。凡此皆危急，多不得脱。商人重番僧，云度海危难祷之，则见于空中，无不获济，至广州饭僧设供，谓之「罗汉斋」。

Imperial decree: Large sea vessels had hundreds of people on board, and small ones had more than a hundred. Among them, the leading merchant was designated as Gangshou (leader), Fugangshou (deputy leader), or Za Shi (miscellaneous officer). The Shiyi Shi (Maritime Trade Office) would issue a red seal for identification, allowing these leaders to use canes to discipline their crew members. In cases of death among the crew, their property was registered and taken into account. Merchants said that they dared to go only when the ship was large and had many people on board. There were many pirates overseas, who would rob ships not heading to their country. For example, if a vessel bound for Chancheng (Champa) lost its way and mistakenly entered Zhelai (Javaka), all of its cargo would be seized, and the crew members from the north would be tied up and sold, with the pirates saying: "You were not originally coming to this place." Although foreign countries may have no commercial taxes, they often made demands, called "xiangsong" (tribute or offerings). Regardless of the amount of cargo, these demands were imposed uniformly. Therefore, small ships found it disadvantageous to engage in such trade. Merchant ships were dozens of zhang deep and wide, with merchants dividing the space to store their goods. Each person was allotted about several chi of space, using the lower part for storage and sleeping on top at night. The cargo consisted mostly of ceramic wares, with large and small items nested together, leaving no empty space. At sea, people did not fear wind and waves, but only feared running aground, which was called "Coushan." Once this happened, the ship could no longer be freed. If a ship suddenly developed a leak, it was impossible to repair from inside. Therefore, they ordered "guinu" (slave divers) to hold knives and felt material to patch the hole from outside. These guinu were skilled swimmers who could enter the water with their eyes open. The ship captain knew the geography; at night, he observed the stars, and during the day, he watched the sun. On overcast or hazy days, he relied on the south-pointing compass. Sometimes, they would use a ten-zhang rope with a hook to pull up mud from the seabed and smell it—this allowed them to determine their location immediately. There is no rain at sea; whenever there is rain, it means that land or mountains are near. Merchants said that when merchant ships encountered calm weather with no wind, the sea water was as still as a mirror. The sailors fished using a large hook as big as an arm, tying a chicken or duck as bait. They would let it sink until a large fish swallowed it; then they followed the fish for half a day until it grew tired. After getting slightly closer, they waited another half-day before finally catching it. If suddenly wind arose, they had to abandon the catch. Sometimes they caught large fish that were not edible; they would then open their bellies to extract the smaller, edible fish inside. A single belly could contain no fewer than dozens of small fish, each weighing about ten jin. Large sea fish often followed the ships up and down; whatever was thrown into the water, they would devour without exception. Sailors who fell ill feared dying on board the ship; therefore, they often wrapped a person whose breath had not yet ceased in thick mats and threw them into the water, hoping they would sink quickly. To ensure this, several clay jars filled with water were tied between the mats. However, as soon as they were thrown overboard, schools of fish would swarm and devour both the mats and the body, so that it sank hardly at all. There was a sawshark (ju sha) that could be hundreds of zhang long, with a nasal bone shaped like a saw. When it encountered a merchant ship, it would cut through the vessel horizontally as if slicing rotten wood. When a merchant ship was sailing at sea, if the crew suddenly saw what appeared to be a mountain of withered wood piled up in the distance, and the captain suspected that there had been no such landform there before, they believed it to be a jiaolong (a mythical dragon-like creature). In response, they would cut off their hair, take fish scales and bones, burn them together as an offering, and gradually the apparition would disappear into the water. All of these were perilous emergencies, from which many could not escape. Merchants highly valued foreign monks, believing that if they prayed to them for safe passage through the dangerous sea journey, these monks would appear in the sky and no one failed to be rescued. Upon arriving at Guangzhou, merchants would offer food and make offerings to the monks; this was called "Luohan Zhai" (Bodhisattva's Alms).

5 北人过海外，是岁不还者，谓之「住蕃」；诸国人至广州，是岁不归者，谓之「住唐」。广人举债总一倍，约舶过回偿，住蕃虽十年不归，息亦不增。富者乘时畜缯帛陶货，加其直与求债者，计息何啻倍蓗。广州官司受理，有利债负，亦市舶使专敕，欲其流通也。

Northern Chinese who went overseas and did not return that year were called "Zhu Fan" (settling among the foreigners); people from various foreign countries who came to Guangzhou and did not return home that year were called "Zhu Tang" (settling in the Tang land). The people of Guangzhou, when taking out a loan, would agree to repay double the amount. They would make an agreement that it would be repaid once the merchant ship returned from its voyage. Even if someone who had "Zhu Fan" (settled overseas) did not return for ten years, no additional interest was added. The wealthy would take advantage of the opportunity to stockpile silk, brocade, and ceramic goods, raising their prices for those seeking loans. Calculating the interest in this way, it was not merely double but many times more. The officials of Guangzhou handled such matters and, for debts that were profitable, also issued special imperial orders through the Shiyishi (Director of Maritime Trade), in order to encourage circulation.

6 广州蕃坊，海外诸国人聚居，置蕃长一人，管勾蕃坊公事，专切招邀蕃商入贡，用蕃官为之，巾袍履笏如华人。蕃人有罪，诣广州鞫实，送蕃坊行遣。缚之木梯上，以藤杖挞之，自踵至顶，每藤杖三下折大杖一下。盖蕃人不衣褌裤，喜地坐，以杖臀为苦，反不畏杖脊。徒以上罪则广州决断。蕃人衣装与华异，饮食与华同。或云其先波巡尝事瞿昙氏，受戒勿食诸肉，至今蕃人但不食猪肉而已。又曰汝必欲食，当自杀自食，意谓使其割己肉自啖，至今蕃人非手刃六畜则不食，若鱼鳖则不问生死皆食。其人手指皆带宝石，嵌以金锡，视其贫富，谓之指环子，交址人尤重之，一环直百金，最上者号猫儿眼睛，乃玉石也，光焰动灼，正如活者，究之无他异，不知佩袭之意如何。有摩娑石者，辟药虫毒，以为指环，遇毒则吮之立愈，此固可以卫生。

The Fanfang (foreign quarter) of Guangzhou was where people from various overseas countries resided. A single Fanzhang (foreign leader) was appointed to oversee public affairs in the Fanfang, specifically responsible for inviting foreign merchants to pay tribute. This position was filled by a foreign official who wore headgear, robes, shoes, and tablets just like Chinese officials. If foreigners committed crimes, they would be brought to Guangzhou for investigation; once the facts were established, they would be sent back to the Fanfang (foreign quarter) for punishment. The offender was tied onto a wooden ladder and beaten with rattan sticks, from the soles of the feet up to the head. For every three strokes with a rattan stick, it counted as one stroke with a heavy rod. This was because foreigners did not wear underpants or trousers and preferred to sit on the ground; thus, they found being beaten on the buttocks particularly painful, yet strangely feared being flogged on the back less. For crimes more serious than corporal punishment, Guangzhou authorities would make the final judgment and sentencing. The clothing and attire of foreigners differed from that of the Chinese, but their food and diet were the same as those of the Chinese. Some say that their ancestors, Boxun, once served the Qutan Shi (Buddhist lineage) and received precepts forbidding them from eating all kinds of meat. To this day, however, foreigners only abstain from eating pork. It was also said, "If you really want to eat, you must kill and eat yourself," meaning that they were supposed to cut their own flesh and consume it. To this day, foreigners will not eat livestock unless they have personally slaughtered them with their own hands; however, for fish and turtles, whether alive or dead, they will eat without distinction. Their fingers were all adorned with gemstones, set in gold and tin. The value of these ornaments reflected their wealth or poverty; they were called "zhuanguanzi" (ring stones). People from Jiaozhi especially valued them highly—each ring could be worth a hundred jin of silver. The most prestigious ones were known as "maoer yanjing," which was actually a kind of jade and stone, glowing so brightly it seemed alive. Upon closer examination, however, there was nothing particularly unusual about them; no one really understood the meaning or tradition behind wearing these ornaments. There was a kind of stone called Mosha Shi, believed to repel medicinal pests and toxins. It was made into rings; if one encountered poison, sucking on it would immediately bring about recovery. This could indeed be considered beneficial for health preservation.

7 海南诸国，各有酋长，三佛齐最号大国，有文书，善算。商人云，日月蚀亦能预知其时，但华人不晓其书尔。地多檀香、乳香，以为华货。三佛齐舶赍乳香至中国，所在市舶司以香系榷货，抽分之外，尽官市。近岁三佛齐国亦榷檀香，令商就其国主售之，直增数倍，蕃民莫敢私鬻，其政亦有术也。是国正在海南，西至大食尚远，华人诣大食，至三佛齐修船，转易货物，远贾辐凑，故号最盛。

The various states of Hainan each had their own chieftains, among whom Sanfoqi was considered the greatest kingdom. It had written records and its people were skilled in mathematics. Merchants said that they could also predict the time of solar and lunar eclipses in advance, but it was simply that Chinese people did not understand their script. The land is rich in sandalwood and frankincense, which were considered valuable goods for trade with the Chinese. Ships from Sanfoqi brought frankincense to China, and wherever they arrived, the local Shiyi Shi (maritime trade offices) treated the incense as a state-monopolized commodity. After collecting their share through taxation, all remaining goods were officially purchased by the government. In recent years, Sanfoqi also placed a state monopoly on sandalwood, requiring merchants to sell it through the country's ruler. The price was increased several times over, and no foreign residents dared to privately trade it; this demonstrated that their governance had its own methods as well. This country lies directly south of Hainan. The distance from it to Dashi (Arabia) was still far. Chinese merchants traveling to Dashi would arrive at Sanfoqi for ship repairs and the exchange of goods, making this place a hub for long-distance traders who gathered in large numbers; thus, it became known as the most prosperous region.

8 广中富人，多畜鬼奴，绝有力，可负数百斤。言语嗜欲不通，性淳不逃徙，亦谓之野人。色黑如墨，唇红齿白，发鬈而黄，有牝牡，生海外诸山中。食生物，采得时与火食饲之，累日洞泄，谓之换肠。缘此或病死，若不死，即可蓄。久蓄能晓人言，而自不能言。有一种近海野人，入水眼不眨，谓之昆仑奴。

Wealthy people in Guangzhou often kept "guinu" (slave divers or strong laborers), who were extremely powerful and could carry hundreds of jin. They did not understand the local language or indulge in desires, had a simple nature, and would not run away or migrate; they were also called "yeren" (wild people). They had black skin like ink, red lips, white teeth, curly yellow hair. They had both male and female genders and were born in the mountains of overseas regions. They ate raw food; when first captured, if fed cooked food, they would suffer from severe diarrhea for several days, a process known as "huan chang" (changing the intestines). Because of this, some would fall ill and die; if they survived, however, they could then be kept as slaves. Those who had been kept for a long time could gradually understand human speech, but were themselves unable to speak. There was one kind of wild people living near the sea who could dive into water without blinking their eyes; they were called Kunlun Nu (Kunlun slaves).

9 广州杂俗，妇人强，男子弱。妇人十八九，戴乌丝髻，衣皂半臂，谓之「游街背子」。

The customs in Guangzhou are mixed, with women being strong and men being weak. Most women in their late teens or early twenties wore a black silk coiled hairstyle, dressed in half-sleeve black garments known as "Youjie Beizi."

10 乐府有「菩萨蛮」，不知何物，在广中见呼蕃妇为「菩萨蛮」，因识之。

There is a musical piece called "Bosaman" in the Yuefu (Music Bureau) collection, but no one knows exactly what it refers to. In Guangzhou, I saw foreign women being called "Bosaman," and thus I came to understand its meaning.

11 广州蕃坊，见蕃人赌象棋，并无车马之制，只以象牙、犀角、沈檀香数块，于棋局上两两相移，亦自有节度胜败。予以戏事，未尝问也。

In the Fanfang of Guangzhou, I saw foreigners playing xiangqi (Chinese chess), but their version had no pieces for "chariots" or "horses." Instead, they used only a few pieces made of ivory, rhinoceros horn, and sandalwood on the board, moving them in pairs back and forth. Yet there was still a system to determine victory and defeat according to its own rules. I regarded it as just a game and never paid much attention to it.

12 馀在广州，尝因犒设，蕃人大集府中。蕃长引一三佛齐人来，云善诵《孔雀明王经》。馀思佛书所谓《真言》者，殊不可晓，意其传讹，喜得为证，因令诵之。其人以两手向背，倚柱而呼，声正如瓶中倾沸汤，更无一声似世传《孔雀真言》者。馀曰其书已经重译，宜其不同，但流俗以此书荐亡者，不知中国鬼神如何晓会。

While I was in Guangzhou, I once hosted a banquet for rewards, during which many foreigners gathered at the government office. The Fanzhang brought a man from Sanfoqi and said he was skilled in reciting the "Kongque Mingwang Jing" (Peacock King Sutra). I thought of the Buddhist scriptures referred to as "Zhenyan" (true words or mantras), which I had never fully understood, and suspected that their transmission might have been corrupted. Delighted at this opportunity for verification, I asked him to recite it. The man raised both his hands, one forward and one backward, leaned against a pillar, and shouted. His voice was exactly like boiling water being poured from a jar; not a single sound resembled the version of the "Kongque Zhenyan" (Peacock Mantra) that had been passed down in the world. I said that the text must have undergone multiple translations, which explained why it was different. However, since common people in China used this scripture to offer prayers for the deceased, I wondered how Chinese spirits and deities could possibly understand or recognize it.

13 南海庙前有大树，生子如冬瓜，熟时解之，其房如芭蕉，土人呼为波罗蜜，渍之可食。

Before the Nhai Temple there was a large tree that bore fruit resembling winter melons. When ripe, if one cut it open, its interior would look like a banana's pulp. The local people called it "bolomi," and after soaking, it could be eaten.

14 英州碧落洞生锺乳，牧羊者多往焉。或云羊食锺乳间水，有全体如乳白者，其肉大补羸，谓之乳羊。活时了不能识，刲之然后见，极难得，或一岁得一二枚，郡守即献广帅、监司。

The Biluo Dong (Jade Drop Cave) in Ying Zhou produces stalactites; many shepherds often go there. Some say that sheep drinking water from the area where stalactites form may become entirely white, like milk; their meat is said to greatly benefit the weak and frail. These are called "ru yang" (milk sheep). When alive, they look entirely unremarkable and cannot be identified; only after slaughtering them does one see the distinctive features. They are extremely rare—sometimes only one or two might be found in a year—and upon discovery, local officials would immediately present them to the Guangzhou military commander and the supervisory officers.

15 汉以神雀改元，书传不言其状。广南人说神雀，或红或白，一群必备五色，飞集极高树，自十丈以下，皆不肯栖，食露吸风，网罟不能及。馀在曹溪寺屡见之，忽来倏去，嘲唽似雀噪，色鲜明，询诸彼人，自来未尝有捕得者。

The Han dynasty changed its era name because of a divine quail (shenque), but historical records do not describe what it looked like. The people of Guangnan describe the shenque (divine quail) as either red or white, and in any flock, all five colors must be present. They fly and perch only on very tall trees—never below ten zhang—and will not settle lower. They feed on dew and sip the wind; their height makes them impossible to catch with nets or snares. While I was at Caoxi Temple, I saw them many times—suddenly appearing and then vanishing quickly. Their calls were raucous like those of sparrows, and their colors were vividly bright. When I asked the locals about them, they said that no one had ever successfully captured one before.

16 海南诸国有倒挂雀，尾羽备五色，状似鹦鹉，形小如雀，夜则倒悬其身。畜之者食以蜜渍粟米、甘蔗。不耐寒，至中州辄以寒死；寻常误食其粪，亦死。元符中，始有携至都城者，一雀售钱五十万，东坡《梅》词云：「倒挂绿毛么凤。」盖此鸟也。

The various states of Hainan have a kind of bird called the "daoguaque" (upside-down hanging quail), whose tail feathers display all five colors. It resembles a parrot in appearance, is small like a sparrow, and at night it hangs its body upside down. Those who kept these birds fed them with honey-soaked millet and sugarcane. They could not tolerate the cold, and upon arriving in Zhongzhou (Central China), they would often die from the chill. Even accidentally eating their droppings could prove fatal. In the Yuanyu era, some were first brought to the capital city; one such bird could be sold for fifty thousand coins. Su Shi (Dongpo) mentioned it in his ci poem "Plum Blossom," writing: "An upside-down hanging green-feathered little phoenix." This refers to that bird.

17 馀在广州，购得白鹦鹉，译者盛称其能言。试听之，能蕃语耳，嘲唽正似鸟声，可惜枉费教习，一笑而还之。

While I was in Guangzhou, I purchased a white parrot; the interpreter greatly praised its ability to speak. I tested it and found that it could only speak in foreign languages, its sounds raucous and bird-like. It was a pity to have wasted effort on training it; I returned it with a smile.

18 南方大龟，长二三尺，介厚而白，造玳瑁器者用以补衬，名曰龟筒。方谚曰：「龟筒夹玳瑁，鬼神不晓会。」初时民间无用，不可售，后缘官市，价踊贵。先公帅广，内侍省牒广州市龟筒数百斤，公不报。僚吏以为言，公曰：「吾专行之，勿累尔矣。」卒不与市，民赖以不扰。

Large turtles in the south can grow two to three chi long, with thick and white shells. These are used as linings or supports when making tortoiseshell wares; they are called "guigong" (turtle tubes). A local proverb says: "When turtle tubes are inlaid with tortoiseshell, even ghosts and gods cannot understand it." At first, these were of no use to the common people and could not be sold; later, due to official demand, their price soared. My late father was in command of Guangzhou when the Office of Eunuchs issued a notice requesting hundreds of jin of turtle tubes from the Guangzhou market. My father did not respond to it. The subordinates and clerks brought this matter up with him, but my father said: "I will handle this personally; do not trouble yourselves over it." In the end, he did not proceed with the purchase, and thus the people were spared from disturbance.

19 广右英州清远峡小龙祠，馀尝谒之，数间屋当溪山奇绝处。龙乃五虵：其色一如生金，王也；一如红锦，妃也；一青一绿，判官也；一黄，走吏也；又有小者如王色，太子也。蟠曲一漆合中，发视之，或见或隐，甚神异。其状比常虵细颈而长，横目广颡，不畏人，色皆鲜明，胜于丹青，祀之则出据香炉上，火不能爇，或食所祀酒茗。

To the right of Guangzhou lies Ying Zhou, where in Qingyuan Xia there is a Xiaolong Temple. I once paid homage to it; its few rooms were situated at an extraordinary and scenic spot along the stream and mountains. The dragon was said to be composed of five snakes: one with a color like raw gold, representing the king; another red as brocade, symbolizing the queen; one green and one blue, representing attendants; one yellow, signifying an official scribe; and there was also a smaller one resembling the king's color, representing the crown prince. They were coiled together inside a lacquered box. When opened to look, they would sometimes appear and at other times remain hidden; it was considered very supernatural and strange. Their appearance was similar to ordinary snakes, but with slender necks and elongated bodies, wide-set eyes, and broad foreheads. They were not afraid of people, and their colors were all vivid and more brilliant than paintings. When worshipped, they would emerge and perch on the incense burner; fire could not burn them, and sometimes they would even eat the wine or tea offered in worship.

20 闽、浙人食蛙，湖湘人食蛤蚧，大蛙也。中州人每笑东南人食蛙，有宗子任浙官，取蛙两股脯之，给其族人为鹑腊，既食然后告之，由是东南谤少息。或云蛙变为黄?。广南食蛇，市中鬻蛇羹，东坡妾朝云随谪惠州，尝遣老兵买食之，意谓海鲜，问其名，乃蛇也，哇之，病数月，竟死。琼管夷人食动物，凡蝇蚋草虫蚯蚓尽捕之，入截竹中炊熟，破竹而食。顷年在广州，蕃坊献食，多用糖蜜脑麝，有鱼虽甘旨，而腥臭自若也，唯烧笋菹一味可食。先公使辽日，供乳粥一椀甚珍，但沃以生油，不可入口。谕之使去油，不听，因绐令以他器贮油，使自酌用之，乃许，自后遂得淡粥。大率南食多盐，北食多酸，四夷及村落人食甘，中州及城市人食淡，五味中唯苦不可食。

People from Min and Zhe provinces eat frogs; those from Huxiang eat gejie, which are large frogs. People from Zhongzhou often laughed at those in the southeast for eating frogs. Once, a member of the imperial clan who held an official post in Zhe took two legs of a frog and dried them as 脯 (cured meat), giving it to his relatives as quan la (preserved pheasant). After they had eaten it, he informed them of its origin, and from then on, the criticism against southerners for eating frogs somewhat subsided. Some say that frogs can transform into huangtu (yellow toad). In Guangnan, people eat snakes; snake soup is sold in the market. When Dongpo's concubine Chao Yun was exiled to Huizhou with him, she once sent an old soldier to buy food, thinking it was seafood. When she asked for its name and learned that it was a snake, she vomited violently, fell ill for several months, and eventually died. The native people of Qiongguan eat all kinds of animals; they catch flies, mosquitoes, grass insects, and earthworms, place them inside cut bamboo tubes, steam them until cooked, then break open the bamboo to eat. A few years ago in Guangzhou, the foreign quarter presented food, much of which used sugar, honey, musk, and civet. Although some fish dishes were delicious, they still retained their usual fishy smell; only one dish, roasted bamboo shoots pickle, was edible. When my late father was on a mission to Liao, he was served one bowl of milk porridge considered very precious. However, it was simply poured with raw oil and could not be eaten. He was advised to remove the oil, but they did not listen. My father then deceived them by asking them to store the oil in another vessel so that they could pour it themselves as desired; only then were they willing to comply. From then on, he was able to obtain a plain, unsalted porridge. In general, southern food is heavily salted, while northern food tends to be sour. People from the border regions and villages prefer sweet flavors, whereas those from Zhongzhou and urban areas favor mild tastes. Among the five basic flavors, bitterness alone is considered inedible.

21 广州医助教王士良，元佑元年，死三日而苏。自言被追至冥府，有衣浅绛衣如仙官者据殿，引问士良尝为人行药杀妻，士良不服。有吏唱言「是熙宁四年始」，即取籍阅，良久云「并无」。仙官拊案曰：「本是黄州，误做广州。」令放士良还。既出，又令引至庑下，有揭示云:「明年广南疫，宜用此药方。」士良读之，乃《博济方》中钩藤散也，本方治疫。士良读之，乃窃询左右：「此何所也？」或言太司真人，治天下医工。时蔡元度守五羊，闻之，召士良审问，令幕客作记。及春，疫疠大作，以钩藤散治之，辄愈。士良又云：「幼习医，至熙宁四年方用药治病，冥冥中已记录，可不慎哉！」

Wang Shiliang, an assistant medical instructor in Guangzhou, died three days after the first year of Yuanyou and then revived. He claimed that he had been taken to the underworld, where a man dressed in light crimson robes like an immortal official sat on the hall and questioned Wang Shiliang about having once prescribed medicine that caused a wife's death. Wang denied this accusation. An official shouted, "It began in the fourth year of Xining," and immediately took out the records to check. After a long while, he said, "There is nothing there." The immortal official struck the desk and said: "It was originally Huangzhou, mistakenly written as Guangzhou." He ordered Wang Shiliang to be released back to life. After leaving, he was again led to a side hall, where there was a notice displayed that read: "There will be an epidemic in Guangnan next year; it is advisable to use this medicine formula." Wang Shiliang read it and realized that it was the Gou Teng San from the Boji Fang, a formula originally used to treat epidemics. Wang Shiliang read this and secretly asked those around him, "What place is this?" Some said it was Tai Si Zhenren, who oversees all physicians in the world. At that time, Cai Yuandu was governing Wuyang (Guangzhou), and upon hearing of this, he summoned Wang Shiliang for questioning and ordered his staff to record the account. When spring arrived, an epidemic broke out, and those treated with Gou Teng San were promptly cured. Wang Shiliang also said: "I began studying medicine as a child, and it was not until the fourth year of Xining that I actually used medicine to treat illness. Even in the unseen world, these deeds have already been recorded—how cautious one must be!"

22 元佑间，广州蕃坊刘姓人娶宗女，官至左班殿直。刘死，宗女无子，其家争分财产，遣人挝登闻院鼓。朝廷方悟宗女嫁夷部，因禁止，三代须一代有官，乃得取宗女。

During the Yuanyou period, a man of the Liu family from the foreign quarter in Guangzhou married into the imperial clan and was appointed to the post of Zuoban Dianzhi. After Liu died, his wife from the imperial clan had no children, and members of her family quarreled over dividing the property. They sent someone to strike the drum at Dengwenyuan (the Office for Reporting Grievances) to lodge a complaint. The court then realized that an imperial daughter had married into the foreign community, and thus prohibited such marriages, stipulating that for three generations, at least one member must hold an official post before being eligible to marry an imperial daughter.

23 邹浩志完，以言事得罪贬新州，媒孽者久犹不已。元符二年冬，有旨付广东提刑锺正甫就新州鞫问志完事，不下司。是时锺挈家在广州观上元灯，得旨即行。漕帅方宴集，怪其不至，而已乘传出关矣，众愕然。锺驰至新，召志完，拘之浴室。适泰陵遗诏至，锺号泣启封；志完居暗室，不自意得全，又闻使者哭泣，罔测其事，意甚陨获。良久，锺遣介传语，止言为国恤不及献茶，且请归宅。志完亦泣而出。其后东坡闻之，戏云：「此茶不烦见示。」

Zou Hao Zhiwan, having offended the court by speaking out on matters, was punished and exiled to Xin Zhou. Those who sought to persecute him continued their schemes for a long time without ceasing. In the second year of Yuanfu, during winter, an imperial decree was issued ordering Zhong Zhengfu, the Guangdong Inspector of Justice, to personally investigate Zhiwan's case in Xin Zhou without referring it to a lower office. At that time, Zhong Zhengfu was in Guangzhou with his family to watch the Lantern Festival. Upon receiving the imperial order, he immediately set out on his journey. The canal commissioner and military commander were holding a banquet and found it strange that Zhong did not attend, but he had already departed by official conveyance through the pass. Everyone was astonished. Zhong Zhengfu hurried to Xin Zhou, summoned Zhiwan, and detained him in a bathhouse. At that moment, an imperial edict from Tai Ling arrived. Zhong Zhengfu wept as he opened the seal; Zhiwan was confined in a dark room and did not expect to be spared. Upon hearing the messenger weeping, he could not fathom what had happened and felt greatly alarmed and confused. After a long while, Zhong sent an attendant to deliver a message, merely stating that due to national mourning, tea could not be offered, and requesting Zhiwan's return home. Zhiwan wept as he left. Later, Dongpo heard about this and joked, "This tea need not be shown to me."

24 东坡元丰间知湖州，言者以其诽谤时政，必致死地，御史台遣就任摄之，吏部差朝士皇甫朝光管押。东坡方视事，数吏直入上厅事，捽其袂曰：「御史中丞召。」东坡错愕而起，即步出郡署门，家人号泣出随之。弟辙适在郡，相逐行及西门，不得与诀，东坡但呼：「子由，以妻子累尔！」郡人为之泣涕。下狱即问五代有无誓书铁券，盖死囚则如此，他罪止问三代。东坡为一诗付狱吏，他日寄子由，其诗曰：「圣主如天万物春，小臣愚暗自亡身。百年未满先偿债，十口无归更累人。是处青山可埋骨，他时夜雨独伤神。与君世世为兄弟，更结来生未了因。」狱吏怜之，颇宽其苦楚。狱成，神考薄其罪，止责散官，安置黄州。元佑中，复起为两制用事。绍圣初，贬惠州，再窜儋耳。元符末，放还，与子过乘月自琼州渡海而北，风静波平，东坡叩舷而歌，过困不得寝，甚苦之，率尔曰：「大人赏此不已，宁当再过一巡？」东坡矍然就寝。馀在南海，逢东坡北归，气貌不衰，笑语滑稽无穷，视面多土色，靥耳不润泽。别去数月，仅及阳羡而卒。东坡固有以处忧患，但瘴雾之毒，非所能堪尔。

During the Yuanfeng period, Dongpo served as the governor of Huzhou. Those who accused him claimed that he had defamed current policies and would surely face execution. The Office of Censorate sent officials to arrest him upon his arrival in office, and the Ministry of Personnel assigned Chao Shi Chao Guang to supervise and escort him. Dongpo was just beginning his official duties when several clerks suddenly entered the main hall, grabbed his sleeve, and said: "The Vice Minister of Censorate has summoned you." Dongpo was startled and rose at once. He immediately walked out through the gates of the prefectural office, with his family wailing and following behind him. Dongpo's younger brother, Zhe, happened to be in the prefecture at that time and hurried after him as far as the western gate. Unable to say a final farewell, Dongpo simply called out: "Ziyou, I leave my wife and children to you!" The people of the prefecture wept at the scene. When he was imprisoned, they immediately asked whether there were any sworn documents or iron vouchers from the Five Dynasties period. This was because such procedures applied only to death-row prisoners; for other crimes, they inquired only about three generations. Dongpo composed a poem and gave it to the jailer, to be sent later to his brother Zhiyou. The poem read: "The sage ruler is as vast as heaven, bringing spring to all things; a petty official like me, ignorant and obscure, has brought about my own downfall." A hundred years not yet fulfilled, I must first repay my debts; ten mouths without a home will only burden others. These green hills here can bury my bones; in some future night, rain will grieve me alone. Let us be brothers life after life, and bind anew the karmic ties yet unfulfilled in this world." The jailer took pity on him and somewhat eased his suffering. When the case was concluded, Emperor Shenzong deemed his crime minor, imposing only a demotion from official rank and ordering him to be placed in Huangzhou. During the Yuanyou era, he was reinstated and rose again to prominence as an imperial court official. At the beginning of the Shaosheng era, he was demoted to Huizhou, and later exiled once more to Danye. At the end of the Yuanfu era, he was pardoned and allowed to return. Accompanied by his son Su Guo, they traveled northward under the moonlight from Qiongzhou across the sea. The winds were calm and the waves still; Dongpo tapped on the ship's railing and sang joyfully. Su Guo, however, was exhausted and unable to sleep, finding it very trying. He remarked offhandedly: "Father keeps enjoying this endlessly—should I sing another round for you?" Dongpo started, then went to bed. I was in Nanhai when I encountered Dongpo returning north. His spirit and appearance had not diminished; he spoke with endless humor and mirth. Yet his face bore a dusty hue, and his ears lacked luster. A few months after we parted, he had barely reached Yangxian when he passed away. Dongpo indeed possessed the fortitude to endure adversity, but the toxicity of the malarial mist was something he could not withstand.

25 孙权破曹操于赤壁，今沔、鄂间皆有之。黄州徙治黄冈，俯大江，与武昌县相对。州治之西距江，名赤鼻矶，俗呼鼻为弼，后人往往以此为赤壁。武昌寒溪，正孙氏故宫，东坡词有「人道是周郎赤壁」之句，指赤鼻矶也。坡非不知自有赤壁，故言「人道是」者，以明俗记尔。

Sun Quan defeated Cao Cao at Chibi; today, places named Chibi can be found between Mian and E prefectures. Huangzhou moved its administrative seat to Huanggang, overlooking the great Yangtze River, facing Wuchang Xian directly across. To the west of the seat of Huangzhou is a place at some distance from the river, named Chibi Ji. The local people commonly refer to "bi" (nose) as "Bi," and later generations often took this place for the site of the Red Cliff. Wuchang Huanxi is where Sun Quan's former palace stood. In Dongpo's ci poetry, there is the line: "People say this is Zhou Lang's Red Cliff," referring to Chibi Ji (Red Nose Shoal). Dongpo was certainly aware that there was an actual Red Cliff, and thus he said "People say it is..." to clarify that this was merely a popular misconception.

26 东坡在黄州，手作菜羹，号为「东坡羹」，自叙其制度，好事者珍奇之。

While in Huangzhou, Dongpo personally made vegetable soup, which he named "Dongpo Geng." He himself described its ingredients and method of preparation, and those with a taste for such things regarded it as a rare delicacy.

27 宫殿置鸱吻，臣庶不敢用，故作兽头代之，或云以禳火灾。今光州界人家屋皆兽头，黄州界惟官舍神庙用之，私居不用，云恐招回禄之祸。相去百里，风俗便不同。

Palaces are adorned with chiwen (chimney-like ridge ornaments in the shape of mythical creatures), which commoners and officials dare not use; thus, they made substitute animal-shaped heads instead. Some say this was done to ward off fires. In the present day, in the area of Guang Zhou, every household's roof features animal-shaped heads; but in Huangzhou territory, only official buildings and temples use them—private residences do not, as it is said that using them might invite misfortune from "Hui Lu" (a term associated with calamity or retribution). Though only a hundred li apart, the customs differ.

28 三月上巳祓禊，其来亦远。寒食禁火，主介子推，河东之俗也。江浙民间多竞渡，亦有龙舟，率用五月五日，主屈原，湘楚之俗也。二者皆尚贤，而末流则害教，晋人寒食病老幼，楚人竞渡致鬬讼。

The Shangsi Festival in the third month, with its ritual of purification by water, has a long historical tradition as well. The Hanshi Festival, during which fire is forbidden, honors Jie Zitui and originates from the customs of Hedong. In Jiangsu and Zhejiang, many common people compete in dragon boat races; they also use dragon boats, usually on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month. This custom honors Qu Yuan and originates from Xiangchu (Hunan-Hubei) traditions. Both customs honor virtuous men, but their later developments have harmed moral teachings: the people of Jin became ill from overexertion during Hanshi Festival, affecting both young and old; while in Chu, dragon boat races led to quarrels and lawsuits.

29 忠洁侯者，屈原也。大观间议开直河，省洞庭迂险，使者沈延嗣总其事，辟属官。有勾当公事卢供奉，过湖溺死。或传旁舟见鬼物出波间，云：「吾血食此，若由直河，则将安仰！」馀以忠洁侯当无此言，傥以其兴不可成之功，徒殚民力，则毙之亦三闾遗意也。

Zhongjie Hou is Qu Yuan. During the Daguan period, there was a proposal to excavate a straight canal to shorten and ease the winding and dangerous route through Dongting Lake. The envoy Shen Yanshi was put in charge of this project and appointed subordinate officials. A certain Gongshang Gongfu Lu, an official in charge of public affairs, drowned while crossing the lake. Some said that people on nearby boats saw ghostly figures emerging from the waves, who declared: "I have long feasted upon blood here; if you dig a straight river, where shall I look for offerings!" I believe Zhongjie Hou (Qu Yuan) would not have said such words. Perhaps, however, it was because he opposed the attempt to accomplish an impractical feat that only drained the people's strength; in dying thus, he followed the legacy of the Three Lus [the loyal followers who drowned themselves after Qu Yuan].

30 馀客沔、鄂，闻人说张乖崖初为崇阳令，至今血食，父老犹能道其政事。尝逢村氓，市菜一束出郭门，问之则近郊农家，乖崖笞之四十，曰：「尔有地而市菜，惰农也。」崇阳民闻之，相尚力田。乖崖一日遣吏尽伐民间茶园，谕令更种桑柘，民失茶利，甚困，然素畏服其政令，不敢慢。乖崖代去数年，会朝廷更榷法，园户纳茶租钱，崇阳独无茶园，免输。邑去郡四百里，不通舟楫，岁输，一夫负米至郡，每斛率得六七斗，富者租百斛，甚为劳费。乖崖使三司建言，高原县分苗米折纳绢，崇阳民遂得轻赍，而先植桑柘已成，蚕丝之利甲于东南，迄今尤盛。

While I was a guest in Mian and E, I heard people speak of Zhang Guaiyai when he first served as magistracy of Chongyang. To this day, he is venerated with blood sacrifices; the elders still recount his governance. I once encountered a peasant carrying a bundle of vegetables as he left the city gate. When asked, it turned out to be a farmer from the nearby countryside. Guaiyai flogged him forty times and said: "You have land yet buy vegetables—this makes you a lazy farmer." The people of Chongyang heard about this and came to value diligent farming. One day, Guaiyai sent officials to cut down all the tea gardens in the countryside, instructing the people to replant them with mulberry and zhe trees. The locals lost their income from tea and suffered greatly; yet they had long respected his authority and dared not disobey. Several years after Guaiyai left office, the imperial court revised its monopoly laws. Tea garden households were required to pay rent in money for their tea harvests; but since Chongyang alone had no tea gardens, it was exempt from this payment. The county was four hundred li from the prefectural seat, with no access by boat. Each year, taxes had to be transported on foot; one man carrying rice to the prefecture would typically arrive with only six or seven dou for every hu he set out with. For those who rented a hundred hu of grain, this was an extremely laborious and costly burden. Guaiyai instructed the Three Bureaus to submit a proposal, suggesting that in highland counties like Chongyang, rice taxes be converted into silk payments. As a result, the people of Chongyang were relieved from the heavy burden of transporting grain. Moreover, by then the mulberry and zhe trees planted earlier had already matured; the profits from silkworms surpassed those of any region in the southeast, and this prosperity has continued to the present day.

31 黄州董助教甚富。大观己丑岁歉，董为饭以食饥者，又为糗饵与小儿辈。方罗列分俵，饥人如墙而进，不复可制，董仆于地，颇被欧践。家人咸咎之，董略不介意。翌日又为具，但设阑楯，以序进退，或时纷然，迄百馀日无倦也。黄冈村氓闾丘十五，多积谷，每幸凶岁即腾价，细民苦之。老年病且亟，不复饮食，但餐羊屎。家人怜之，以米饵作羊屎状绐之，入手便投去，唯食真者。数月方死。此氓媚佛，多施庐山僧供积，亦内惧祸至，冀事佛少逭责，此尤不可也。

Dong Zhujiao of Huangzhou was very wealthy. In the year Gouchou of Dagan, when there was a poor harvest, Dong Zhujiao prepared rice to feed the starving people and also made dried cakes for the children. Just as they were arranging to distribute the food, the starving crowd surged forward like a wall and could no longer be controlled. Dong fell to the ground and was somewhat beaten and trampled. His family members all blamed him for this, but Dong Zhujiao paid no heed whatsoever. The next day he prepared food again, but this time set up barriers and rails to regulate the flow of people. Though there were occasional disturbances, for over a hundred days he persisted without tiring. A peasant named Luqiu Shiwu from Huanggang was known for hoarding grain, and whenever a bad harvest year came, he would raise prices; the common people suffered greatly under this. In his old age, he fell seriously ill and could no longer eat or drink normally, but only consumed sheep droppings. His family pitied him and made rice cakes shaped like sheep droppings to deceive him, but as soon as he touched them, he threw them away, only eating the real ones. He died several months later. This peasant was devoted to Buddhism and had given much alms to monks on Lushan Mountain. Yet, inwardly he feared retribution for his wrongdoing, hoping that by practicing devotion to the Buddha, he might escape blame—this is especially unacceptable.

32 黄冈民丁生微，稍稍有生事，性桀黠，遂致富，创买田宅。治井得片石，肤脉成字，如其姓名，丁即模刻，令士人作碑记实。未几病死，家旋破，馀售之，今萍洲是也。田庐似是前定，当有以受之，不尔未见能享者。

A commoner named Ding Shengwei from Huanggang was originally of humble means but gradually became enterprising. By nature, he was fierce and cunning, which led him to wealth, enabling him to purchase land and houses. While digging a well, he found a piece of stone with natural patterns forming characters resembling his name. Ding immediately had it carved and commissioned a scholar to compose an inscription recording the event as if it were authentic. Not long after, he fell ill and died; his family soon afterwards declined into poverty. The stone was later sold, and it is now in Pingzhou. Land and dwellings seem to be predestined; one must have the merit to receive them, otherwise, none has been seen who could truly enjoy such blessings.

33 黄鲁直再谪黔中，泊舟武昌，初和甫追饯之。相与处舟中，岸巾危坐，鲁直侧席，意甚恭。犹子无咎与黄士潘观来，不知其为初和甫，忽略之。潘、黄正论《本草》，反复良久。鲁直曰：「吾侄前！识初和甫否？」二人缩舌汗背。

Huang Luzhi was exiled to Qianzhong for the second time and moored his boat in Wuchang, where Chu Hefu went after him to see him off with a farewell banquet. They stayed together on the boat; Hefu removed his cap and sat upright at the edge of the boat, while Huang Luzhi sat slightly aside on a mat, showing great respect. His nephew Wujiu and Huang Shipan Guan came along as well, but they did not recognize Chu Hefu and ignored him. Huang Shipan and his companion were discussing the "Bencao" (Compendium of Materia Medica), going back and forth for a long time. Huang Luzhi said, "My nephew, come forward!" "Do you recognize Chu Hefu?" The two men were stunned into silence, their tongues drawn back and sweat beading on their backs.

34 汉威令行于西北，故西北呼中国为汉；唐威令行于东南，故蛮夷呼中国为唐。崇宁间，臣僚上言：「边俗指中国为唐、汉，形于文书，乞并改为宋。」谓如用唐装汉法之类，诏从之。余窃谓未宜，不若改作华字，八荒之内，莫不臣妾，特有中外之异尔。

The authority of the Han dynasty prevailed in the northwest, so people in the northwest referred to China as "Han." The authority of the Tang dynasty prevailed in the southeast, so the southern and eastern barbarians called China "Tang." During Chongning, officials submitted a proposal: "The border peoples refer to China as Tang or Han in their documents; we respectfully request that these be changed uniformly to Song." It was explained that this included terms such as "Tang zhuang" (Tang costume) or "Han fa" (Han law), and the imperial edict approved the request. I secretly thought this was not advisable; it would have been better to change them instead to the word "Hua." Within all eight directions, none are not subjects of China; there is only a difference between the inner and outer regions.

35 辽人嗜学中国。先朝建天章、龙图阁以藏祖宗制作，置待制、学士以宠儒官；辽亦立乾文阁，置待制、学士以命其臣。典章文物，仿效甚多。政和壬辰，朝廷得元圭，肆赦；是冬，辽亦称得孔子履，赦管内。

The Liao people were fond of studying Chinese culture. The previous dynasty established the Tianzhang and Longtu Ge to house the works of ancestors, and appointed officials such as Dai Zhi (Attendant) and Xueshi (Scholar) to honor Confucian scholars; the Liao also founded Qianwen Ge, and set up positions for Dai Zhi and Xueshi to bestow upon their own ministers. In terms of regulations, ceremonial objects, and cultural artifacts, the Liao imitated the Chinese system extensively. In the Renchen year of Zhenghe, the imperial court obtained a yugui (a ceremonial jade scepter), and thus issued an amnesty; that winter, the Liao also claimed to have found Confucius's sandals and granted amnesty within their territory.

36 先公言使北时，见北使耶律家车马来迓，毡车中有妇人，面涂深黄，谓之「佛妆」，红眉黑吻，正如异物。或说人眉在眼上，设有眉在眼下者，众必骇见。使人人眉在眼下，而忽见眉在眼上者，其骇亦尔。故天下未尝有正论，杂然如此。要之世间事不可立异，且须通俗。

My late father said that when he was sent on a mission to the north, he saw northern envoys from the Yelü family coming to meet him with carriages and horses. Inside one of the felt-covered wagons there were women whose faces were painted deep yellow; this was called "Fozhuang" (Buddha Makeup). They had red eyebrows and black lips, looking just like strange creatures. Some said that human eyebrows are above the eyes; if there were people with eyebrows below their eyes, others would surely be startled to see them. If everyone had eyebrows beneath the eyes and suddenly someone appeared with eyebrows above their eyes, they would also be startling to behold. Therefore, throughout the world there has never been a single correct view; things are mixed and varied like this. In short, matters in the world should not be made peculiar or different; they must also be popular and conventional.

37 北地产鹿，有倍大于中国者，鹿角近根实处，刻以为环，肉好相半，内虚可贮物，谓之鹿顶合。

Northern regions produce deer, some of which are much larger than those in China. Near the base of their antlers, where the flesh is solid, they carve it into rings; half of each ring consists of meaty parts and half is hollow, capable of holding objects. These are called "ludinghe" (deer-antler caps).

38 京师置都亭驿待辽人，都亭西驿待夏人，同文馆待高丽，怀远驿待南蛮。元丰待高丽人最厚，沿路亭传皆名高丽亭。高丽人泛海而至明州，则由二浙遡汴至都下，谓之南路；或至密州，则由京东陆行至京师，谓之东路。二路亭传一新。常由南路，未有由东路者，高丽人便于舟楫，多赍辎重故尔。

The capital city established Duting Yizhi (Duting Post) to accommodate the Liao people, Duting Xiyi (Western Duting Post) for the Xia people, Tongwen Guan (Hall of Unified Script) for Goryeo envoys, and Huaiyuan Yi (Huaiyuan Post) for southern barbarian visitors. During Yuang 丰, the treatment of Goryeo envoys was especially generous; all roadside pavilions and relay stations along the route were named "Gaoli Ting" (Goryeo Pavilion). Goryeo envoys arriving by sea reached Mingzhou, then traveled upstream along the two Zhe regions via Bian River to the capital; this route was called the Southern Route. Alternatively, if they arrived at Mizhou, they would proceed overland through Jingdong to the capital city, which was known as the Eastern Route. The pavilions and relay stations on both routes were all newly renovated. They usually took the Southern Route, none had ever taken the Eastern one; this was because Goryeo envoys preferred water travel and often brought large amounts of supplies.

39 高句骊，古箕子之国，虽夷人能文。先公守润，得其使先状云：「远离桑域，近次蔗封。」盖取食蔗渐入佳境之义。崇宁中，遣使贺天宁节，表有「良月就盈」之句，盖谓十月十日，其属辞如此。

Goguryeo, an ancient state of Ji Zi, though a people of the barbarians, was capable of literature. My late father served as governor of Runzhou and received a letter from their envoy which read: "Far away from mulberry fields, now staying near the sugarcane frontier." This was an allusion to gradually entering a better situation through the sweetness of sugarcane. In the Chongning period, an envoy was sent to congratulate on the Tianning Festival. The memorial presented contained the line "the good month reaches its fullness," referring to the tenth day of the tenth lunar month; their phrasing was thus.

40 高丽人尝在常州，买民间养鸽放之，鸽识家飞去，常人唯恐不售。使还，又托生辰买鸽放生，人家争出鸽。既售，即笼入舟中，去更数日，方生辰，遂载行，反以为得计。

Goryeo envoys once stayed in Changzhou and bought pigeons raised by local residents, then released them. The pigeons recognized their homes and flew away; the locals were only afraid that they would not be able to sell their pigeons. When the envoy returned, he again asked on behalf of his birthday for people to buy pigeons and release them as an act of merit; thus, families competed to bring out their pigeons. After the pigeons were bought, they were caged and placed on board the ship. Several days later, when it was finally his birthday, they released them while traveling, considering this a clever plan.

41 九江之下贵池口，属池州，九江之上富池口，属兴国军。富池口有吴将甘宁庙，案《吴志》，甘宁死于当口，或疑其富池口也，又恐自有当口。宁传云：「为西陵太守，以阳新下雉为奉邑。」今永兴县有阳新里下雉村，盖宁故国。庙碑刻甚多，并无说此者。

Below Jiujiang is Guichi Kou, which belongs to Chizhou; above Jiujiang is Fuchi Kou, belonging to Xingguo Jun. There is a temple of Wu general Gan Ning at Fuchi Kou. According to the Wu Zhi (Records of Wu), Gan Ning died at Dangkou; some suspect that this refers to Fuchi Kou, yet others fear there may be another place called Dangkou on its own. Gan Ning's biography states: "He served as Tai Shou of Xiling, with Yangxin Xi zhi designated as his fengyi (hereditary territory)." Today, Yongxing Xian has a village called Yangxin Li Xia Zhi Cun; this is likely Gan Ning's former homeland. There are many inscriptions on the temple steles, but none mention this.

42 东海神庙在莱州府东门外十五里，下瞰海咫尺，东望芙蓉岛，水约四十里。岛之西水色白，东则色碧，与天接。岛上有神庙，一茅屋，渔者至彼则还。屋中有米数斛，凡渔人阻风，则宿岛上，取米以为粮；得归，便载米偿之，不敢欺一粒。稍北与北蕃界相望，渔人云，天晴时夜见北人举火，度之亦不甚远。一在蓬莱阁西，后枕溟海。

The Temple of the Eastern Sea Deity is located fifteen li outside the eastern gate of Laizhou Fu. It overlooks the sea within arm's reach, and to the east one can see Furong Dao (Fragrant Lotus Island), about forty li away across the water. To the west of the island, the waters are white; to the east, they turn emerald green, merging with the sky. There is a deity temple on the island, consisting of only one thatched hut; fishermen who arrive there return immediately. Inside the hut, there were several hu of rice. Whenever fishermen were stranded by bad weather, they would stay on the island and take some rice as food; upon returning safely, they would bring rice to repay what had been taken, not daring to be dishonest even by a single grain. Slightly to the north, it faces the northern barbarian territory; fishermen say that on clear nights they can see fires lit by people from the north, and judging by this, the distance is not very great. One [temple] lies to the west of Penglai Ge, with the boundless sea stretching behind it.

43 先公守东莱，派买上供绵十万两，诸邑请重禁私市，公曰：「如是将扰而不能办。」问：「市价几钱？」曰：「每两百钱。」公命增二十，委掖令田望莅之如私市，贮钱邑门，不问多少，随手交易。十馀日，四乡趋利而来，遂足所售数。或谓价外增直，恐亏有司，公曰：「朝廷平价和市之意正如此。」

My late father served as the governor of Donglai and was ordered to purchase 100,000 liang of tribute-quality wool. The various counties requested strict prohibitions on private trade, but my father said: "If we do so, it will cause disturbances without successfully completing the task." He asked: "What is the market price?" They replied, "One hundred coins per liang." The official ordered an increase of twenty coins, making it one hundred and twenty per liang. He entrusted Tian Wang, the magistrate of Ye, to oversee the purchase as if in a private market, storing the money at the gate of the county seat without specifying amounts, allowing transactions freely as they occurred. Within 十多 days, people from all four surrounding towns flocked there for profit, and the required amount was soon fulfilled. Some said that increasing the price beyond market value might harm official interests, but my father replied: "This is precisely what the court intends by purchasing at fair prices and ensuring smooth transactions."

44 崇宁初行当十大钱，秤重三小钱。后以币轻物重，令东南改为当五钱，轻于东北，私铸盗贩不可禁，乃一切改为当三，轻重适平，然后定。是时内帑藏钱无算，折阅万亿计。京师一旦自凌晨，数骑走出东华门，传呼里巷，当十改为当三，顷刻遍知。故凡富人，无所措手。开封府得旨，民间质库，限五日作当十赎质。细民奔走趋利，质者不堪命，稍或拥遏，有司即以重刑加之。有巨豪善计者，至官限满，自展五日，依旧作当十赎质，大榜其门，朝廷闻而录赏之。馀族父炳居湖州仪凤桥西，常贮数百缗钱以射利。会当十法变，子弟先得消息，请速以钱易他货，族父笑而不答，良久云：「钱遂不可用耶？」子弟曰：「然。」族父曰：「我不用，他人亦不可用，又何为？」既失此，后稍不给，终不少悔。

In the early Chongning period, the Dangshi Daqian coins were put into circulation, each weighing three small qian on a scale. Later, because the coins were light while goods were expensive, an order was issued for the southeast to change them into "Dangwu" (five-fold) coins, which were lighter than those in the northeast. Smuggling and private casting could not be stopped, so they were uniformly changed to "Dangsān" (three-fold) coins, with weight adjusted to a balanced level, after which things stabilized. At that time, the imperial treasury held an immeasurable amount of money, with losses and depreciation counted in the tens of billions. One morning in the capital, just before dawn, several horsemen burst out of Donghua Gate and shouted through the streets and alleys that Dangshi coins would be changed to Dangsān. In a moment, the news spread everywhere. Thus, all wealthy people were at a loss as to what to do. The Kaifeng Fu received imperial orders: private pawnshops in the civilian sector had five days to redeem pledged items using Dangshi coins. The common people rushed about in pursuit of profit, while those who had pledged items could hardly bear the burden. If there was any slight obstruction or delay, officials immediately imposed severe punishments upon them. A wealthy and shrewd individual, upon the official deadline passing, voluntarily extended it by five days, continuing to redeem pledged items with Dangshi coins as before. He posted a large notice at his door announcing this. The imperial court heard of this and rewarded him accordingly. My uncle by marriage, Bing, lived west of Yifeng Qiao in Huzhou and often stored several hundred strings of coins to seek profit. When the Dangshi coin policy changed, his younger relatives learned of the news first and urged him to quickly exchange the coins for other goods. My uncle-in-law smiled but did not reply; after a long while he said: "Have these coins suddenly become unusable?" The younger relatives replied, "Yes." My uncle-in-law said: "If I do not use them, others cannot either. Then what is the problem?" After losing this opportunity, later when money became slightly scarce, he never showed any regret.

45 州郡承唐衰藩镇之弊，颇或僭拟，衙皂有子城使、军中使、教练使等号，近制始革去。先公知润州，值衙校转资，用黄纸写牒，公大惊，吏白旧例，其间尽准敕条。通判州事慎宗杰以为无害，公曰：「岂有庶官而敢押黄纸耶？」自后改用白纸。故事：中书门下侍郎、宰相押黄，后省官皆押纸背。慎在常调，未尝知此。

The prefectures and commanderies, inheriting the corrupt practices of the declining Tang dynasty's military circuits (fanzhen), often engaged in overstepping their authority. Their official ranks included titles such as Zicheng Shi (Governor of Inner City), Junzhong Shi (Military Envoy), and Jiaolian Shi (Training Officer). These were abolished only with recent reforms. My late father served as magistrates of Runzhou when a military officer was being promoted. The officials used yellow paper to write the official document, which greatly surprised my father. The clerks explained that this followed established precedent and fully conformed to imperial regulations. Tongpan Zhou Shi Shen Zongjie thought it harmless, but my father said: "How can a minor official dare to use yellow paper for an official document?" From then on, they changed to using white paper. According to established practice: the Zhongshu Menxia Shilang (Minister of the Secretariat and Chancellery) and chancellors used yellow paper for their official seals, while officials from lower departments all sealed on the back of ordinary paper. Shen Zongjie was an ordinary official in regular appointment and had never known this custom.

46 阳翟田望，勤于竿牍，亦善其事，日发数十函不倦，由此自出官移令，改秩出常调，皆自致也。一书用好纸数十幅，近年纸价高，田俸入尽索于此。亲朋间目之为「纸进纳」，盖纳粟得官号「进纳」，故以名之。

Tian Wang of Yangdi was diligent in handling official documents and also skilled at his duties, processing dozens of letters each day without tiring. Because of this, he personally drafted official orders and changed ranks beyond the regular appointments—achievements entirely due to his own efforts. A single document could use dozens of sheets of fine paper; in recent years, with high paper prices, officials' salaries were entirely consumed by this expense. Friends and relatives jokingly referred to it as "zhi jinnai" (paper tribute). This was because the system of obtaining official ranks by contributing grain was called "jinnai," so they gave this name in jest.

47 近年拳石之贵，其直不可数计。太平人郭祥正旧蓄一石，广尺馀，宛然生九峰，下有如岩谷者，东坡目为「壶中九华」，因此价重，闻今已在御前。东坡集中载《怪石供》，云谪居黄时所得。馀寓居其地，屋后有山，名破湖山，乃此石所出处也。每年潦水退，细民往求之，五色莹彻，中有缠丝者，可琢为环珥玩饰，常苦其细，置斛中渍水养菖蒲，不适他用。

In recent years, the value of quanshi (a type of stone) has become extremely high, its price beyond calculation. A native of Taiping, Guo Xiangzheng, once owned a stone over one chi in width, which resembled the Nine Peaks so vividly that there were even features resembling grottoes and valleys beneath it. Dongpo (Su Shi) named it "Huzhong Jiuhua" ("Nine Lotus Mountains in a Vase"), thereby greatly increasing its value; I hear it is now before the imperial court. In Dongpo's collected works, there is an essay titled "Guaishi Gong" (Offering of a Strange Stone), in which he mentions that it was obtained during his exile to Huangzhou. I once lived there as a guest, and behind my house was a mountain called Poku Shan; this is the place where such stones originate. Every year, after the floodwaters receded, common people would go to search for them. These stones are clear and luminous in five colors; some have twisted threads inside, suitable for carving into rings, earrings, or ornaments. People often found them too small for such purposes, so they placed them in hu (measuring vessels) soaked with water to grow chongyangpu (sweet flag), not using them for anything else.

48 刘鋹好治宫室，欲购怪石，乃令国中以石赎罪。富人犯法者，航海于二浙买石输之。今城西故苑药洲有九石，皆高数丈，号「九曜石」。

Liu Hui was fond of building palaces and wished to purchase strange stones, so he ordered that within his state, people could use stones as payment for the redemption of crimes. Wealthy individuals who had broken laws would sail to the two Zhe regions (Zhejiang) to buy stones and send them as payment for their crimes. Today, in the old garden of Yaozhou to the west of the city, there are nine stones, each several zhang tall, known as "Jiuyao Shi" (Nine Constellation Stones).

49 端州石在深谷中，细而润。初为官封之，已难得；后兴庆建军，以王地禁采石，不复可得。石上有鸜鹆眼，宛若生者，晕多而青绿为贵，磨砻终不可去，俗传透石涎也。端砚藏久无不甈者，以石润，久亦干，故不平，如湿木乾则不平。

The stone from Duanzhou is found in deep valleys and is fine-grained yet smooth. Initially, it was controlled by official seals for collection; at that time, it was already rare. Later, when Xingqing established a military garrison, the area was declared royal land and stone gathering was banned—after that, it became impossible to obtain. There are patterns on the stone resembling the eyes of a quding (a kind of bird), looking as if alive. Those with more halos and shades of blue-green are considered most valuable; no amount of polishing can remove them, and it is commonly believed that they were formed by the saliva seeping through the stone. Duan inkstones, if stored for a long time, will inevitably become uneven. This is because the stone is smooth and retains moisture; over time it dries out and becomes uneven, just like wet wood that dries and warps.

50 造笔用兔毫最佳，好事者用栗鼠须或猩猩毛以为奇，然不若兔毫便于书也。广南无兔，用鸡毛，虽毛匾不可书，代匮而已。近世笔工，宣州诸葛氏，常州许氏，皆世其家。安陆成安道、弋阳李展之徒，尚多驰名于时。宣人善治竹管，莹洁可爱，亦有以苇为管者，贵其轻。高丽使过常州市笔，诸许待其解舟，即急售之，半无毛头，以为得计。

Horsehair brushes made from rabbit hair are the best; those who take an interest in refined things sometimes use bristles of chestnut mice or red ape hairs to consider them rare, yet they are not as convenient for writing as rabbit hair. Guangnan has no rabbits, so chicken feathers are used instead; although the brushes made from them are flat and unsuitable for writing, they are merely substitutes in times of scarcity. In recent times, brush makers such as the Zhuge family from Xuanzhou and the Xu family from Changzhou have all passed down their craft through generations. People like Cheng Andao of Anlu and Li Zhan of Yiyang were also widely renowned in their time. The people of Xuan are skilled at making bamboo tubes, which are clear and clean and very lovely; some also use reeds for the tubes, valuing their lightness. When envoys from Goryeo passed through Changzhou to purchase brushes, the various Xu families would wait until they moored their boats and then hurriedly sell them, many of which had no bristles at all; they considered this a clever strategy.

51 叶涛好弈棋，介甫作诗切责之，终不肯已。弈者多废事，不论贵贱，嗜之率皆失业，故唐人目棋枰为「木野狐」，言其媚惑人如狐也。

Ye Tao was fond of playing Go; Jiefu composed a poem to sternly reprimand him, but he never ceased his passion. Many who play Go neglect their duties; regardless of rank or status, those addicted to it often lose their jobs. Hence, the people of the Tang dynasty referred to the chessboard as "Mu Yehu" (wooden fox), saying that it seduces and deceives people like a fox does.

52 自崇宁复榷茶，法制日严，私贩者因以抵罪，而商贾官券，请纳有限，道路有程，纤悉不如令，则被系断罪，或没货出告缗，愚者往往不免。其侪乃目茶笼为「草大虫」，言其伤人如虎也。

Since the resumption of tea monopoly during Chongning, laws and regulations became increasingly strict. Those who privately traded in tea were punished accordingly; merchants with official permits could only purchase a limited amount, had to follow set routes and schedules. If they failed to comply fully with these rules, they would be detained and sentenced, or their goods confiscated and fines imposed. Often, the foolish could not avoid punishment. Their peers thus referred to tea caddies as "Cao Datong" (grass tiger), saying that they harm people like tigers do.

53 江西瑞州府黄糵茶，号绝品，士大夫颇以相饷。所产甚微，寺僧园户竞取他山茶，冒其名以眩好事者。黄鲁直家正在双井，其自言如此。

The Huangni tea from Ruizhou Fu in Jiangxi is renowned as an unparalleled product, and scholars and officials often exchanged it with one another. The yield of this tea is very limited; temple monks and garden households would eagerly collect tea from other mountains, falsely claiming it as Huangni to deceive those with refined tastes. Huang Luzhi's family was exactly in Shuangjing; he himself stated this.

54 陈州芍药花殊胜，近岁进花，自陈三百里一日一夜驰至都下。其法：初翦花时，用蜜渍蒲黄蘸其疮，微曝之，俟花嫣，乃入笥中；取时刈去所封蒲黄，布湿地上一两时顷，絣绳以花倒悬之，真如新采者。

The peony flowers of Chen Zhou are exceptionally fine; in recent years, when flowers were presented to the capital, they were transported from Chen over a distance of three hundred li and arrived at the imperial city within one day and night. The method is as follows: when first cutting the flowers, use honey-soaked pu huang (a type of pollen) to apply on the cut ends; then slightly expose them to sunlight. Once the flowers bloom beautifully, place them in baskets. When collecting, remove the sealed pu huang and spread the flowers on a damp ground for one or two hours. Then tie them with ropes and hang them upside down—the result is as fresh as newly picked flowers.

55 抚州莲花纱，都人以为暑衣，甚珍重。莲花寺尼凡四院造此纱，捻织之妙，外人不可传。一岁每院才织近百端，市供尚局并数当路，计之已不足用。寺外人家织者甚多，往往取以充数，都人买者，亦自能别寺外纱，其价减寺内纱什二三。

The lotus flower gauze from Fuzhou was considered by the people of the capital as summer clothing, and it was highly valued. The nuns from four courtyards in Lianhua Temple produced this gauze; the skill of their spinning and weaving was so exquisite that outsiders could not replicate it. Each year, each courtyard produced barely more than a hundred duan; the demand from the imperial supply bureau and several high-ranking officials far exceeded this amount, making it already insufficient for use. Outside the temple, many households wove similar fabric and often used it to fill in; however, people in the capital who purchased it could easily distinguish between the temple-made gauze and that made outside. The price of the latter was lower by about twenty or thirty percent compared to the temple's product.

56 两川冶金，沿溪取沙，以木盘淘，得之甚微，且费力。登、莱金坑户，止用大木，锯剖之，留刃痕，投沙其上，泛以水，沙去，金著锯绞中，甚易得。元佑中，莱州城东刘姓茔地金苗生，官莅取焉。乃发墓，凡砖瓦间皆金色也。刘葬才十数年，不知气脉蒸陶如此之速。累月取尽，地为深穴，得金万亿计，自官抽官市、匠吏窥窃外，刘所得十二三焉。京东诸郡之钱尽券与刘氏，刘氏乃一村氓不分菽麦者，得钱无所用，往来诸郡，恍忽醉饱，岁馀亦死，钱竟没官，刘世遂绝。

The people from the two Shu regions who smelted metal would follow along streams, collect sand, and use wooden trays to pan for it; what they obtained was very small in quantity, and the process was also laborious. The gold-mining households in Deng and Lai used only large wooden boards, sawed them open, left the blade marks, spread sand on top, rinsed it with water—the sand would wash away, leaving the gold adhering to the sawn grooves; this method made obtaining gold very easy. During Yuanyou, a cluster of gold veins emerged in the Liu family's burial ground to the east of Laizhou city; officials came and extracted it. They then exhumed the tomb, and found that between every brick and tile, there was a golden hue. The Liu family had been buried there for only several decades; no one knew the vital energies could transform it into gold so quickly. For months they extracted until it was all taken, leaving the site a deep pit; the amount of gold obtained was counted in tens of thousands. Apart from what officials collected through official purchases and artisans or clerks stole secretly, the Liu family received only about one-tenth to one-fifth of it. All the money from various prefectures in Jingdong was given to the Liu family; yet the Liu were merely villagers who could not distinguish between beans and wheat. Having no use for the money, they traveled among the prefectures, dazed and drunk on feasts. After more than a year, they died, and all the money eventually reverted to official possession—thus the Liu lineage came to an end.

57 崇宁间，邓州南阳县村民发古冢，县尉王俨莅掩之。王为馀言其详，云竁中有二瓦棺，已碎其左者，购得一铜印，方寸许，篆文甚古，识之者云「温不禁印」。时方竞访古器，即为中贵人取去，未知温何代人也。仲父久中尚奇，每仿古物，立怪名，以绐流俗。庐于先茔下，山多岩谷，乃披荆棘求其壮观者，刻取前人题署、姓名、年号，皆诡异，既不可据，真儿戏尔。前人所居与其器用，后世所以爱慕之者，思其人焉。其人无可思而宝其物与地者，蔽也。夫冥器儿戏，又乌足以为君子之雅好也欤！

During Chongning, villagers from Nanyang Xian in Deng Zhou unearthed an ancient tomb; the county 尉 Wang Yan arrived to cover it up. Wang told me in detail, saying that there were two earthen coffins inside the tomb; the one on the left had already been broken. They purchased a bronze seal about an inch square, with ancient seal script characters. Those who could recognize it said it was the "Wen Bujin Yin" (Seal of Wen Bujin). At that time, people were eagerly seeking ancient artifacts; the seal was immediately taken away by a eunuch of high rank. To this day, it remains unknown who Wen Bujin was or from which dynasty he came. My uncle Zhongfu Jiuzhong loved the unusual for a long time; he often imitated ancient objects, gave them strange names, and used them to deceive the common people. He built a hut beneath the ancestral tomb; since the mountains were filled with rocky valleys, he would cut through brambles in search of impressive sights. He carved out inscriptions left by previous people—names, titles, and reign years—which all appeared strange and bizarre. Since none could be verified as authentic, they were nothing more than childish playthings. The dwellings and utensils of former generations are cherished by later people out of longing for those who once used them. Those who treasure the objects or places without any reason to admire the person involved are simply deluded. Such funerary trinkets and childish playthings—how could they possibly be considered refined tastes worthy of a gentleman!

58 中官宋用臣，熙宁间备任使，以敏练称上意，性极精巧。元佑时，责官舒州，州将作乐鼓甚巨，饰以金彩。既成，其旁一环脚断，欲剖之，惜工费。宋乃献计为环，其下作锁须状，以铁固鼓腹之竁，使甚隘，即钉环入竁中，既入，锁须张，遂不复脱。事多似此。

The eunuch Song Yongchen, during the Xining era, was entrusted with various duties and was praised by the emperor for his alertness and competence. He had an extremely meticulous and skillful nature. During Yuanyou, he was assigned to Shuzhou as a demoted official; the local commander there made an enormous musical drum and decorated it with gold and colorful patterns. After it was completed, one of the rings on its side broke off; they wanted to disassemble it but regretted having spent so much labor and expense. Song then proposed a plan to make the ring, designing its base in the shape of interlocking bristles. He used iron to reinforce the narrow cavity at the drum's belly; once the ring was inserted into the cavity, the bristle-like structure would expand and lock it firmly in place, making it impossible for the ring to come loose again. Many matters were like this.

59 东南谓乌啼为凶，鹊噪为吉，故或呼为喜鹊。顷在山东，见人闻鹊噪则唾之，乌啼却以为喜，不知风俗所见如何。

In the southeast, the cry of a crow is considered ominous, while the clamor of a magpie is seen as auspicious; hence, they are sometimes called "Xique" (joyful magpies). Recently in Shandong, I saw people who would spit when they heard a magpie's clamor, yet regarded the cry of a crow as auspicious. I wonder how different customs and perceptions vary among regions.

60 姚佑自言尝任泽州邑尉，郡当太行之喉，官吏有未尝到处，郡将以虎患，遣尉祠之，乃在山巅。姚往宿山下，见居民环屋埋巨木，云以拒虎。稍晚虎出，数十为群，首尾相衔，睥睨庐舍，人畜俱股栗。旦起登山，姚披练推挽而上，至绝顶，得板屋，有石刻，姚致祭摹墨本以归。

Yao You claimed that he once served as a county 尉 in Ze Zhou. The prefecture was located at the throat of Taihang Mountain, and there were places within it where officials had never set foot. Because tigers were a problem, the military commander sent the 尉 to perform a sacrifice, which took place on top of a mountain. Yao stayed overnight at the foot of the mountain and saw local residents encircling their houses with buried large wooden posts, saying they were used to repel tigers. Later that night, the tigers emerged in groups of dozens, their heads and tails interlocked, glaring at the huts and houses; both people and livestock trembled with fear. At dawn, he climbed the mountain; Yao ascended with difficulty, pushing and pulling his way up in a white robe, reaching the summit where he found a wooden hut. Inside were stone carvings, which Yao offered sacrifices to and then copied onto paper before returning home.

61 溱州有虎穴，凡十里许，修谷茂丛斑斓旁午，南北路口行者相集而度，否则遇害。荆州孙伟奇甫刺溱，亲为予道其详。夫市朝固有此地，人或忽之致祸，可不慎哉！

Qin Zhou has a tiger den, about ten li long. The ravine is lined with dense vegetation and colorful undergrowth in disorderly profusion; travelers from the north and south gather at the crossroads to pass through together—otherwise they would encounter danger. Sun Weiqifu, the prefect of Jing Zhou, personally told me in detail about this when he was stationed in Qin Zhou. Indeed, such places exist even within bustling cities and markets; if people neglect them, calamity may follow. How important it is to be cautious!

62 徽宗大观间，京东路民家有牛生麒麟，村人不识，以为怪，击杀之。有司既闻，验问，真瑞物也。乃上奏，因图其形下诸路，俾民间预识其状，或有生者，即重赏购之。

During the Daguan period of Emperor Huizong, a farmer's family in Jingdong Lu gave birth to a qilin (a mythical creature) from their cow; the villagers did not recognize it and regarded it as an omen or monster, so they killed it. The local officials, upon hearing of this, investigated and confirmed that it was indeed a rare and auspicious creature. They then submitted a report to the imperial court, which ordered an illustration of its form sent down to all regions, instructing common people to recognize its appearance in advance. If any such creature were born again, it would be heavily rewarded with a bounty for its retrieval.

63 元佑间，有携海鱼至京师者，谓之海哥。都人竞观，其人以槛置鱼，得金钱则呼鱼，应声而出，日获无算。贵人家传召不少暇。一日，至州北李驸马园，放入池中，呼之不复出，设网罟百计，竟失之。李园池沼雄胜，或云三殿幸其第爱赏，以为披香、太液所不及。海哥，盖海豹也，有斑文如豹而无尾，凡四足，前二足如手，后二足与尾相纽如一。登、莱傍海甚多，其皮染绿，可作鞍鞯。当时都下以为珍怪，蠢然一物，了无他能，贵人千金求一视唯恐后，岂适丁其时乎？

During the Yuanyou period, there was someone who brought a sea fish to the capital; it was called "Haige." The people of the capital vied to see it; the man placed the fish in a cage and, upon receiving coins, would call out to the fish, which would respond by coming forward. In this way, he earned an immeasurable amount each day. Nobles and wealthy families frequently invited him without pause. One day, he arrived at the garden of Li Huma to the north of the prefecture, released the fish into a pond there. After calling for it, the fish no longer emerged; despite setting nets and trying every possible method, they ultimately lost track of it. Li Huma's garden had ponds and marshes of grand beauty; some said that the emperor once visited his residence, greatly admiring it and considering it superior to Pixin or Taiye (imperial gardens). Haige is, in fact, a sea leopard; it has spotted patterns like those of a leopard but no tail. It has four limbs: the front two resemble hands, while the hind two are twisted together with what would have been the tail, forming one continuous structure. Deng and Lai, being coastal regions, have many of these creatures; their hides, when dyed green, can be made into saddle coverings. At that time, the people of the capital regarded it as a rare and wondrous creature. Yet this clumsy animal had no other abilities; wealthy elites paid thousands of gold coins just to see it once, fearing they would be left behind. Was it merely because it appeared at the right moment?

64 沈遘知杭州，号神明之政，吏不能欺。尝以西湖为放生池，禁捕鱼，人无敢取蛙蚓者。

Shen Gou served as governor of Hangzhou and was renowned for his wise and enlightened governance; no official dared to deceive him. He once designated West Lake as a sanctuary for releasing living creatures, prohibiting fishing; not a single person dared to catch frogs or earthworms.

65 九宫山有金星银星鳝，不居水中，凿山者于坚土内得之，悬暴乾，久不坏。其背金银星宛如一具秤，斤两稀密，无纤毫差，秤星十五斤，鳝背星二十斤，枚枚如此。土人收以治风气病，《本草》不载。

The Jiugong Mountains are home to Jinxing and Yinxing shan (star eels), which do not live in water. Miners find them within firm soil while excavating the mountains; when hung out to dry, they remain intact for a long time without decaying. The patterns of gold and silver stars on their backs resemble a complete scale, with the distribution of weight—light or heavy—arranged in precise density, not differing by even the slightest bit. The star pattern on a scale weighs fifteen jin, while that on an eel's back weighs twenty jin; each one is exactly like this. The local people collect them to treat rheumatic ailments, though they are not recorded in the Bencao (Compendium of Materia Medica).

66 孙叔敖杀枳蛇，盖两首蛇也。江南山中蛇，两端皆有头，口目全具，行相牵挽，腹红背黑，长大率如箸。相传是老蚓，两口无舌，不见其开张，正一大蚓尔。恐叔敖所见不如此，或云枳蛇一颈两首，故怪。

Sun Shuao killed a zhi she, which was apparently a two-headed snake. In the mountains of Jiangnan, there are snakes with heads at both ends; each has a complete mouth and eyes. They move by pulling on each other, one red in the belly and black on the back, generally as long as chopsticks. It is said that these are old earthworms; they have two mouths but no tongues, and one never sees them open. They are nothing more than a particularly large earthworm. It is likely that what Sun Shuao saw was not like this; some say the zhi she has one neck with two heads, which is why it seemed strange.《卷三 Volume 3》[查看正文] [修改] [查看历史]

1 先公在讲筵，闻神考言，熊本表章，用印端谨，朱色鲜明，前后无小异。由此受知，遂擢用至两制。近世长吏生日，寮佐画寿星为献，例只受文字，其画却回，但为礼数而已。王安礼自执政出知舒州，生日属吏为寿，或无寿星画者，但用他画轴，红绣囊缄之，必谓退回。王忽令尽启封，挂画于厅事，标所献人名衔于其下。良久，引客爇香，共相瞻礼。其间无寿星者，或用佛像，或用神鬼，唯一兵官所献，乃崔白画二猫，既至前，惭惧失措。或云时有囊缄墓铭者，吏不敢展，此尤失献芹之意，小节不可不戒，古人不欺幽隐，正谓此类。

My late father was at a lecture session when he heard Emperor Shenkao say that Xiong Ben's official documents were well presented, the seals used with due care and precision, the vermilion color bright and clear, and there was no slight difference in quality before or after. Because of this, he gained the emperor's favor and was soon promoted to a position within the two chancelleries. In recent times, when high officials' birthdays arrive, their subordinates and assistants present paintings of the Star of Longevity as gifts. By convention, they only accept written texts; the paintings are returned, serving merely as a formality. Wang Anli, when transferred from a position of state authority to serve as the governor of Shuzhou, had his subordinates present birthday gifts. Some did not offer paintings of the Star of Longevity but instead used other painting scrolls, sealed in red embroidered pouches; these were inevitably returned. The governor suddenly ordered all the seals to be broken, and the paintings to be hung in the hall of official business, with the names and titles of those who had presented them marked beneath each painting. After a long while, he led his guests to burn incense and together they paid mutual respects in solemn ceremony. Among them, those without paintings of the Star of Longevity used instead images of Buddhas or deities and spirits; only a military officer presented a painting by Cui Bai depicting two cats. When it was brought forward, he felt ashamed and fearful, losing his composure. It was said that at the time there were also sealed pouches containing epitaphs, which officials dared not open; this was especially contrary to the intention of offering humble gifts. One must be cautious about such minor details. The ancients did not deceive in hidden matters—this is precisely what they meant by such cases.

2 滕宗闵知楚州，有监司过境，本州岛送酒食，书有臣名，即上闻。既鞫狱，乃书吏误用贺月旦表，无他意，滕坐送吏部监当。盖知州细衔字多，书欲谨，吏每患难写，乘暇用纸写前后衔，谓之空头表笺，用之固已不虔。向宗传为兴国军判官，托士人作与漕使小简，用「金口」、「清光」、「俞允」等字，漕使举行取勘，宛转自解仅免。士人于书尺多不识体要，往往误人，宜谨用，自不能识者，不若不发书。

Teng Zongmin, while serving as the governor of Chuzhou, once had an imperial inspector pass through the region. When the local authorities sent wine and food to entertain him, they included a letter bearing their names as subjects; this matter was immediately reported to the throne. After investigating the case, it turned out that a clerk had mistakenly used a congratulatory memorial for the first day of the month; there was no other intention. Teng Zongmin was therefore punished and assigned to serve under the Ministry of Personnel as an official in charge of oversight duties. The reason is that the official titles of a prefecture governor are long and complicated, so when writing one must be careful. Clerks often found it difficult to write them accurately, so they would take advantage of spare time to write out the full title on paper in advance; this was called "kongtou biao jian" (blank heading memorial paper). Naturally, using such pre-written headings lacked sincerity and reverence. Xiang Zongchuan, serving as a judge-official in Xingguo Jun, entrusted a scholar to draft a short letter addressed to the regional transport commissioner. In it were used such words as "Jinkou," "Qingguang," and "Yuyun." The transport commissioner investigated the matter, and after much back-and-forth explanation, Xiang barely managed to avoid punishment. Scholars often fail to understand the proper conventions in writing short letters, and as a result they frequently make mistakes that mislead others. One should therefore use such forms with care; if one cannot recognize the correct conventions oneself, it is better not to write at all.

3 熙宁中，有常州太守召赴阙，其人颇熟时事，将有陈述，所主亦大臣中有力者，或云介甫。当无不称上意。既陛见，上首问锡山去郡几远。既非素备，了不能对。盖常州无锡县锡山，俗呼惠山，守不阅图经，故不知也。上因顾近臣曰：「作守臣而不知境内山川，其为政可料。」即罢去，竟不曾开陈一言。

The Wanger of Qingzhou once served as the governor of Shuzhou and Danzhou, and his poetry was extremely vulgar. Whenever he presented it to influential officials, those who received it would keep it around merely for amusement and laughter. My uncle once served as an official in Qingzhou, and the Wanger also gave him a scroll of poetry. On another day, when my uncle met his son, he apologized for having accepted it. His son said, "My father, Uncle Ji was speaking nonsense; this has sullied your noble and enlightened character." The common term for someone lacking spirit or vitality is "jiu bo," but how could it be that a thousand would make a sufficient number? My cousin was appointed as Chaoyi Dafu, and through the grace of an eighth-edition amnesty decree, he was promoted to Zhongfeng Dafu. Sima Wengong, while living in leisure at Xijing, once ordered an old veteran to sell the horse he rode. The old veteran secretly laughed at his simplicity, not understanding the depth of his intention.

Fu Zhenggong, after retiring from office, returned to Xidu and often wore a plain hemp robe, riding a donkey outside the suburbs. The public official raised his whip and announced his name: "Bi." The soldier did not understand what was meant and reported to his commander: "A man ahead rode a donkey and rushed through our procession. When we asked for his official rank, he merely said 'Bi.'" 8 世传杜祁公罢相归乡里，不事冠带。一日在河南府客次，道帽深衣坐席末。会府尹出，衙皂不识其故相，有本路运勾至，年少贵游子弟，怪祁公不起揖，厉声问：「足下前任甚处？」祁公曰：「同中书门下平章事。」客次与坐席间固不能遍识，常宜自处卑下，最不可妄谈事及呼人姓名，恐对人子弟道其父兄名及所短者，或其亲知，必贻怒招祸，俗谓口快，乃是大病。

It is said that after Du Qigong was dismissed from the prime ministership and returned to his hometown, he no longer observed formal dress or headgear. One day while staying at a guesthouse in Henan Fu, he sat at the end of the seating area wearing only a simple traveling cap and deep-collared robe. At that time, the governor happened to be away. The attendants did not recognize this former prime minister. When an official from the regional transport office arrived, a young and aristocratic son of a noble family found it strange that Qigong did not rise to bow in greeting. He loudly asked: "Where have you previously served?" Qigong replied, "Minister of the Secretariat and Director of State Affairs." It is naturally impossible for everyone in the guesthouse and at the seating area to recognize each other fully. One should always maintain a humble position. Above all, one must not speak recklessly or call out people's names. One should be especially careful not to mention before others' children the names of their fathers or elder brothers, nor reveal their shortcomings; if such persons are personally known to them, it will surely provoke anger and bring disaster. What common people call "speaking bluntly" is in fact a serious flaw.

9 王荆公退居金陵，结茅钟山下，策杖入村落。有老氓张姓，最稔熟。公每步至其门，即呼「张公」，张应声呼「相公」。一日公忽大咍曰：「我作宰相许时，止与汝一字不同耳！」

Wang Jinggong, after retiring to Jinling, built a thatched hut at the foot of Zhongshan Mountain and walked into villages with his staff. There was an old villager surnamed Zhang who knew him most intimately. Whenever the minister walked to his door, he would call out, "Master Zhang," and Master Zhang would respond by calling him, "Minister." One day, the minister suddenly burst into loud laughter and said: "When I served as prime minister for so long, there was only one character between us that differed!"

10 驸马都尉李端愿，居戚里最号恭慎，既失明，犹戒励子弟，故终身无过。时京师竞传州西二郎庙出圣水，治病辄愈。李素不事鬼神，一日，其子舍有病稚，家人窃往请水，李闻大怒，即杖其子，且云：「使尔子果死，二郎岂肯受枉法赃故活之耶？若不能活，又何求？」

Prince Li Duanyuan, a son-in-law of the imperial family and holding the title of Fu Ma Du Wei, was known among his relatives for being especially respectful and cautious. Even after he lost his sight, he continued to admonish and encourage his descendants, which is why he remained free of fault throughout his life. At that time, the capital was abuzz with rumors that holy water had emerged from the Erlang Temple in the western part of the prefecture, and those who used it for healing were said to be cured immediately. Li had always been indifferent to ghosts and gods. One day, his son took a sick child and secretly went to fetch the holy water. Upon hearing of this, Li became furious and beat his son severely, saying: "If your child were truly destined to die, would Erlang really accept bribes and unjustly spare its life?" "If it cannot be saved, then what is the point of seeking it?"

11 张升杲卿自枢府乞骸，除侍中、河阳三城节度使致仕。幅巾还第，出居阳翟，时时来洛中，游嵩少，颇接方外人，绝口不挂时事。有道人者，善谈虚无，杲卿雅爱之。一日，偕游少室山中，左右从者十馀人。至大松树下，杲卿坐石上，道人探怀出小囊茗屑，汲涧泉、折枯松煮之。杲卿一杯，道人即以馀沥分饮从者，既渴，人竞啜少许，已而皆僵仆。盖茗中置毒药，故以困人，唯道人与杲卿饮者无害尔。道人乃前白曰：「欲告侍中，求随行金银器，往乡市药。」即敛入布囊中，杲卿四顾，左右皆被毒，莫能兴，因大笑遣之携去。至困者醒，药力渐消，始能行，仅至山下，投宿民家。翌日归，乃戒子弟慎交游。

Zhang Sheng Gaoqing requested retirement from the Chushu Fu (Council of Military Secrets) and was granted the title of Shizhong, as well as being appointed as a retired Jiedushi (military governor) of Heyang San Cheng. He returned home in a simple head covering, left the capital to reside in Yangdi, and often came to Luozhong. He traveled between Songshan and Shaoshan mountains, frequently associating with recluses and monks, yet never spoke of current affairs. There was a Daoist recluse who was skilled in discussing the void and emptiness; Gaoqing held him in particular affection. One day, they traveled together into Shaoshi Mountain, accompanied by more than ten attendants and followers. They arrived at the base of a large pine tree, where Gaoqing sat on a stone. The Daoist recluse reached into his robe and took out a small pouch of tea powder, drew water from a mountain stream, broke off some withered pine branches, and brewed the tea. Gaoqing took one cup, and the Daoist immediately divided the remaining tea among his attendants. When they became thirsty, each drank a small amount, and soon afterward, they all collapsed lifelessly. The tea had been laced with poison, intended to incapacitate them; only the Daoist and Gaoqing, who drank it, remained unharmed. The Daoist then stepped forward and said: "I wish to inform Minister Shizhong that I would like to request some gold and silver wares to take with me when I go to the village market to buy medicine." He immediately packed them into a cloth sack. Gaoqing looked around and saw that all his attendants had been poisoned and could not move, so he laughed heartily and sent the Daoist away with the goods. When those who had been incapacitated awoke, the effects of the poison gradually wore off and they were able to move again. They barely made it down the mountain and took shelter in a peasant's home. The next day, upon returning, he gave strict warnings to his descendants to be cautious about their social associations.

12 先公在绍圣初识孟在，盖皇后父也。时泰陵未有嗣，常因景陵宫行香，诸人聚首，孟在忽太息。或询其故，孟曰：「中宫蓐月，满望一皇嗣，乃诞公主！」先公归语所亲曰：「孟在非长守富贵者也！」果如言，后竟废。

My late father first met Meng Zai at the beginning of the Shaosheng era; he was the father-in-law of the empress. At that time, Tai Ling had no heir; he often participated in incense-burning ceremonies at the Jingling Palace, where people would gather. Suddenly Meng Zai let out a deep sigh. Someone asked him the reason, and Meng replied: "The empress is in her confinement after childbirth, hoping for an imperial son, yet a princess was born!" My late father returned home and told his close relatives: "Meng Zai is not the kind of person who can long retain wealth and honor!" As predicted, things turned out exactly as he said; later on, she was indeed deposed.

13 沈起待制诸子，有见荆公者，颇喜之，许以荐擢。一日，沈盛饰出游，过相府，公闻其在门，呼入与共匕箸。先令褫带，沈辞，不得已，公以手褰沈所衣真珠绣直系，连称「好，好」。自后不得复见，坐此沈废。政和中，台章言一朝士，有「湿活居士」之目，谓饮不择酒，内不择人。此数事平时人所易犯，一被指斥，则莫脱，故举以为少俊之戒。

Shen Qi Daidizhi's sons once met Wang Jinggong; he was quite pleased with them and promised to recommend and promote them. One day, Shen dressed up elaborately for an outing and passed by the prime minister's residence. Upon hearing that he was at the gate, the minister called him in to share a meal together. First, he ordered Shen's sash to be removed. Shen protested, but with no choice, the minister himself pulled down the pearl-embroidered belt that Shen was wearing and kept saying, "Good, good." After this incident, he could never meet the prime minister again. Because of this, Shen was dismissed from office. In the Zhenghe era, an official report mentioned a scholar-official who was nicknamed "Shi Huo Jushi," meaning someone who drank without choosing his wine and associated with others without discernment. Therefore, I cite these as warnings for the young and ambitious.

14 张升杲卿微时，与程戡俱下第。槖尽，步出南熏门，至朱仙镇。是日立春，就肆买食，共探怀得数十钱，仅能买汤饼，无钱致肉也，相与摘槐茁荐食而去。后俱在政府，遇立春日，程邀杲卿开宴，水陆毕陈，艳妾环侍，程有骄色。杲卿从容话旧，及朱仙槐角事，程愧其左右，面頳舌咋，终无欢而罢。杲卿归语其内曰：「程三其黜乎？器盈于此矣！」未几，果罢执政。

When Zhang Sheng Gaoqing was still obscure, he and Cheng Kan both failed their imperial examinations together. Their funds exhausted, they walked out through Nanxun Gate and arrived at Zhuxian Zhen. It was the day of Lichun (the Beginning of Spring). They went to a shop to buy food and together searched their pockets, finding only several coins, just enough to purchase some soup noodles. They had no money for meat, so they picked young shoots from a locust tree to eat with their meal before leaving. Later, both of them held high office in the government. On a Lichun day, Cheng invited Gaoqing to a banquet where all kinds of delicacies were laid out, and his beautiful concubines served around him; Cheng wore an air of pride. Gaoqing spoke calmly about the past, mentioning the incident of the locust shoots in Zhuxian. Cheng was shamed before his attendants, his face reddened and he stammered with embarrassment, and the banquet ended without any real merriment. Gaoqing returned home and told his wife: "Will Cheng San be dismissed from office?" His hubris has reached its peak here!" Not long after, Cheng was indeed dismissed from his position of state authority.

15 先公以庆历戊子八月十日生，十八岁，请解于广文馆。尝至汴河上，闻瞽者张听声知祸福，公叩焉。才謦咳，张即曰：「吾故人也！二十年不相遇。」公窃笑其诞。再询，知乡里，便曰：「岂朱秘丞郎君乎？」公愕然，张曰：「庆历八年重阳日，蒙秘丞置酒，次日诣谢，闻公诞弥月，又得预庆宴。秘丞令视公，彼时爱此声，每不忘，屈指已十七年矣。」因道：「公此举未及第，后六年当魁天下。」皆如其言。至今汴河岸常有「张听声」，盖袭其名也。

My late father was born on the tenth day of the eighth month in the year Qingli Wusi. At eighteen, he requested to resign from his post at Guangwen Guan. He once went to the Bianhe River and heard of a blind man named Zhang, who could discern fortune or misfortune by listening to people's voices. My late father inquired about him and paid him a visit. No sooner had he coughed than Zhang immediately said, "This is an old acquaintance of mine!" We have not met for twenty years." My late father secretly laughed at his exaggeration. After further inquiry and learning of his hometown, Zhang immediately said: "Could you not be the son of Master Zhu Mi Cheng?" My late father was astonished. Zhang said, "On the Double Ninth Festival of the eighth year of Qingli, I was honored by Master Zhu Mi Cheng with a banquet. The next day I came to express my thanks and learned that you had just completed your one-month confinement after childbirth; I also had the fortune to attend the celebration feast." 16 馀幼时随母氏在常州，时见钱秀才开图书，知人三世姓，男子知妇姓，女子知夫姓，无不验。吾家之姊，长适吴氏，次适沈氏，钱阅书皆言夫姓吴，当时怪其差缪。后数年，沈姊离婚归宗，嫁吴宽夫，不知图书何为而亿中乃尔。生齿浩繁，岂此数帙文字所能该括？

When I was young, my mother and I were in Changzhou, where I once saw a scholar named Qian who displayed books on physiognomy. He could tell the surnames of three generations of a family; for men, he knew their wives' surnames, and for women, he knew their husbands'. His predictions proved accurate every time. My elder sister from our family first married into the Wu clan, and my younger sister married into the Shen clan. Yet when Qian examined their charts, he claimed both would marry into the Wu family. At that time we found this discrepancy strange and erroneous. Several years later, my sister who had married into the Shen family divorced and returned to her original clan, then remarried to Wu Kuangfu. We could not understand how the book of physiognomy had predicted this outcome so accurately. With such a vast and numerous population, how could these few volumes of text possibly encompass everything?

17 熙宁间，蜀中日者费老筮易，以丹青寓吉凶。在十二辰，则画鼠为子，画马为午，各从其属。画牛作二尾则为失，画犬作二口为哭，画十有一口则为吉，其类不一，谓之卦影，亦有繇词，以相发明。其书曰《轨革》，费老筮之无不验。其后转相祖述，不知消息盈虚者，往往冒行此术，盖中否未可知也，求筮者得幅纸画人物，莫测吉凶，待其相符，然后以为妙。卜以决疑，而转生疑，非先王命卜之意也。其画人物不常，鸟或四足，兽或两翼，人或儒冠而僧衣，故为怪以见象。朝士米芾好怪，常戴俗帽，衣深衣而蹑朝靴、绀缘缬，朋从目为「活卦影」。又开封李昂作卦影，自云能识倚伏，每筮得象，则说谕人，亦有理趣。馀目击一事，曾有一卒持百钱来筮，昂探蓍布卦，即画人裹巾，半衣白，半衣绿，以杖荷二妇人头。昂曰：「卜者士人，半衣白似无官，半衣绿似有官；半绿似无出身，半白又似有出身；荷二妇人头，两头阴，以为贵人之首云。」后询知卜者何大正也。何以布衣上书言元佑皇后称旨得官，后又言元符皇后忤旨失官，卜时方被罪。昂术精妙，馀每求筮，或中或否，不能尽如此。或言日者占筮，系其穷通，所谓术果如何哉！

In the Xining era, in Shu Zhong, a fortune-teller named Fei Lao cast yarrow stalks and used colored paintings to indicate auspiciousness or misfortune. For the twelve earthly branches, he painted a rat for Zi (rat), a horse for Wu (horse), and so on, each according to its respective category. Painting a cow with two tails was considered an omen of misfortune, while painting a dog with two mouths signified weeping; painting an animal with eleven mouths indicated good fortune. There were many such variations, which they called "gua ying" (shadow of the hexagram). Each also had accompanying rhymed verses to further explain their meanings. The book was titled 《 Gui Ge 》, and whenever Fei Lao used it for divination, his predictions proved without fail accurate. Afterward, people successively imitated and followed this practice without understanding the waxing and waning of change. Many rashly practiced this art; whether they hit upon a correct or incorrect result was uncertain. Those seeking divination would receive a piece of paper with painted figures, unable to discern auspiciousness or misfortune, waiting until it matched their situation before considering it miraculous. Divination was meant to resolve doubts, yet in this way it only generates more uncertainty—this is not the original intent of divination as established by our ancient kings. The figures he painted were not conventional; birds might have four legs, beasts two wings, and people sometimes wore Confucian caps with monks' robes—thus creating strange images to convey symbolic meanings. An official named Mi Fu, who was fond of the unusual, often wore a commoner's hat, donned a deep robe while treading in court boots and dark-edged brocade; his companions nicknamed him "The Living Gua Ying." Another person from Kaifeng, Li Ang, created "gua ying," and claimed he could discern hidden dangers and blessings. Whenever he obtained an image through divination, he would explain it to others, and his interpretations were often reasonable and thought-provoking. I personally witnessed one incident: once, a soldier came with a hundred coins to seek divination. Li Ang took the yarrow stalks, cast the hexagram, and immediately painted a figure wearing a headscarf, half-dressed in white and half in green, carrying two women's heads on a staff. Li Ang said: "The diviner is a scholar. Half-dressed in white suggests no official position, while half-dressed in green implies having one; the half-green also resembles lacking an official designation, and the half-white again seems to indicate possessing one. Carrying two women's heads signifies two yin elements, which I take as representing the head of a noble person." Later, upon inquiry, we learned that the diviner was He Dazheng. He, as a commoner without official rank, had once submitted a memorial to the throne about Empress Yuanyou, whose remarks pleased the emperor and earned him an appointment. Later, he spoke ill of Empress Yuanyu, which offended imperial orders and led to his loss of office. At the time of this divination, he was just under punishment for these offenses. Li Ang's art was remarkably refined; whenever I sought his divination, the results were sometimes accurate and sometimes not—I could never achieve such precision as this. Some say that fortune-tellers' predictions of success or failure depend on their skill—so what is the true nature of such arts, after all!

18 文潞公在贝州时，有黄琠者，为公筮。用一幅大绫，写「九十二岁善终」六字，藏于家。考公自二十八岁作两制，知成都；四十二岁平贝州贼，作宰相凡五十馀年。平日未尝降官，虽赎铜罚俸亦无。元佑初，平章军国重事，久之以太师、河东节度使、侍中居西京。绍圣元年，公九十二岁，坐异意降太子少保，河南府差通判来取节钺。月馀终。

When Wen Luxong was in Bei Zhou, there was a man named Huang Dan who cast yarrow stalks for him. Wen Luxong, at the age of twenty-eight, became a high-ranking official and served as governor of Chengdu; at forty-two he quelled rebels in Bei Zhou. He held the position of chancellor for more than fifty years. He had never been demoted from office, and not even once was he punished with a fine in copper or reduction of salary. In the early Yuanyou era, he served as Pingzhang Junguo Zhongshi (a high-ranking official overseeing military and state affairs). After a long time, he held the title of Taishi (Grand Tutor), Hedong Jiedushi (Military Governor of Hedong), and Shizhong (Minister of the Center), residing in Xijing. The government office in Henan Fu sent an official as Tongpan to retrieve his military insignia. He died more than a month later.

19 何执中第五，微时从人筮穷达，其人云：「公不第五否？」何曰：「然。」其人拊掌大笑，连称奇绝，因云：「公凡遇五，即有喜庆。」何以熙宁五年乡荐馀中榜第五人及第，五十五岁随龙，崇宁五年作宰相，每迁官或生子，非五年即五月或五日，其验如此。

In the fifth year of Chongning, he became chancellor. Every time he was promoted to a new post or had a son, it happened either in the fifth year, the fifth month, or on the fifth day—just as predicted.

Qi Shan of Huzhou dreamed at the end of Jiayou that someone wrote a character combining "yu" and "ye" on the side of jade to show him, saying: "This is the emperor's name; this will be the time when you pass the examination." Qi often told many relatives and old acquaintances about this dream. When the emperor ascended the throne in Zhiping, however, his name did not match what he had dreamed. Qi considered the omen to be false. Not many years later, Emperor Shenkao ascended the throne, and his name exactly matched the character from the dream. The townspeople had long heard of the details, and they found it especially miraculous. At that time, Qi had not been selected for the official recommendation, and people were sighing in regret. Suddenly, an imperial decree was issued to extend the age limit and exempt certain candidates from preliminary exams. In Huzhou, only Qi Shan received this exemption, and the following year he passed the provincial examination and successfully entered the ranks of degree holders.

Cai Yuandu's son still realized that in a former life he had been the child of a Wang family from Danyang, Runzhou. When he visited them, it turned out to be true—the wife and children were still there. They came to see him, and they spoke with each other as if no time had passed. When he reached the age of eight or nine, gradually becoming familiar with the ways of this world, he slowly forgot those past-life events. Yongqiu Li Sanli had a daughter named Xiaoshi, who at the age of several years would say: "I was the clerk in Huangping, Huangzhou, responsible for editing and publishing official records." "Lei Ze's son Xiangfu died of a skin disease at the age of seventeen," she said. Niushangduo from Yongqiu had lived in Huangpi, and upon investigation, the details matched perfectly like a seal and its impression. On another day, Lei Ze went to visit Xiaoshi, and at first sight she immediately called him "Father." In the eighth year of Zhenghe, Xiaoshi came to Huangpi, embraced her former mother in grief and weeping. She also spoke with several local residents about events from her past life, all of which proved true.

23 王震子发，平时人相之云：「五十岁水厄。」绍圣二年，责知袁州，五十岁矣。畏水厄，乃陆行至蕲水，疽发顶上，不可救，遂卒。岂所谓水厄者，厄于蕲水耶？

Wang Zhen's son Fa was told by a physiognomist: "You will encounter a water-related disaster at the age of fifty." In the second year of Shaosheng, he was assigned to serve as governor of Yuan Zhou and had reached the age of fifty. Afraid of the water disaster, he traveled overland to Qishui, but a carbuncle broke out on his head and could not be cured; thus, he died. Was the so-called "water disaster" actually an affliction from Qishui?

24 湖州安吉朱斋郎，昔游池州，齐山张道人与之一幅白纸，令寻「青眉子」，云：「刺墨为眉，多作丐者。」朱他日在乡闾，见群丐中有刺青眉者，因叩之。青眉初诟骂，洎朱转与张所寄纸，即笑曰：「张老无恙乎？」先是，涎唾被面，一穷殍耳；既笑，天真粲然，尘不可掩，宛若贵人。良久，谓朱曰：「汝无仙骨，又家富，黄白术不足以相累，有小技可以安乐终天年。」即授之而去。朱自尔大能饮啖，凡四十年无老态。崇宁乙酉，朱病，拏舟入吴兴，将见刘焘。会刘往西安，不能俟，亟呼季父翼中，传其术，语竟引舟归。季父素病，由是康健。不知所谓术者何如也。

Zhu Zailang of Anji, Huzhou, once traveled to Chizhou. A Daoist named Zhang from Qishan Mountain gave him a sheet of white paper and told him to look for "Qingmeizi," saying: "Those who tattoo their eyebrows are often beggars." Later, while in his hometown, Zhu Zailang saw among a group of beggars one with tattooed eyebrows and approached him to inquire. At first, the man with the blue-tattooed eyebrows scolded Zhu rudely. But when Zhu passed him the paper that Zhang had given, he immediately smiled and said: "Is Old Master Zhang well?" Previously, with spittle on his face, he had seemed nothing more than a wretched beggar. But once he smiled, his natural charm shone brightly; the filth could not conceal him—he looked just like an aristocrat. After a long while, he said to Zhu: "You lack the bones of an immortal, and since your family is wealthy, alchemical arts for gold and silver are not worth burdening you with. I have a small skill that will allow you to live in peace and contentment until old age." He then taught him the skill and departed. From that time on, Zhu became greatly capable of drinking and eating heartily; for forty years, he showed no signs of aging. In the year Chongning Bingyou (1105), Zhu fell ill and took a boat to Wuxing, intending to meet Liu Dao. Just then, Liu had gone to Xi'an and could not wait; Zhu hurriedly called his uncle Ji Fu Yizhong and passed on the technique to him. After finishing the transmission, he bid farewell and returned in his boat. Uncle Ji Fu had long been ill; from then on, he regained health and strength. Rao Gong of Fuzhou, before he passed the imperial examination, met a Buddhist monk who told him: "Your future name and position will be exactly like those of Cui Pan Guan from Runzhou." Rao did not believe it at first. Forty years later, Rao Gong was appointed as Tongpan of Runzhou in the rank of Chaqing Lang, which coincided with the time when my late father held the position of governor there. After more than a year in office, while overseeing the renovation of the official hall, Rao Gong discovered an inscribed stone tablet listing the names of former Tongpan officials. On it he saw the name of Cui Pan Guan, with a note stating: "Shifeng Yuanwai Lang (Assistant Official of the Office of Titles), arrived on a certain date and month, and was dismissed on another." Rao Gong joyfully recalled the monk's earlier words and sought out an elderly official to inquire about what had happened after Cui Pan Guan left office. The old clerk replied: "He was replaced and went to Yangzhou, where he passed away." Rao Gong felt a deep inner stirring and immediately submitted a petition to retire. My late father, upon hearing this, strongly advised him against it, but Rao was ultimately unable to be dissuaded.

26 熙宁初，凌运勾权知桂阳监，坐失入死罪废黜。初，桂阳一僧携二徒游庐山，数岁，独其徒归，颇有金帛，日从博饮。僧之姊讼于官，执其徒鞫问，具得僧度牒、衣钵，其徒云：「未至桂阳三十里，江岸大石，同憩其旁。石忽开，有老人召僧入，石复合，至暮候之不出，遂归。」狱中大笑其诞，峻治，竟伏辜，二徒皆坐斩。数月，僧至桂阳，徒家诉冤，官吏由是抵罪。问僧，果入石壁中，见老人，语良久，从地户出，乃在鼎州桃源，僧乞食缓行还乡。事有如此者，至今桂阳监现有案牍。

In the early Xining era, Ling Yun Gou served as acting director of Guiyang Jian (a local administrative office), but was dismissed and disgraced for wrongly sentencing someone to death. At first, a monk from Guiyang brought two disciples to travel in Lushan. After several years, only one of the disciples returned, carrying quite a bit of gold and silk, and he spent his days gambling and drinking. The monk's sister filed a lawsuit with the authorities, and the disciple was arrested for interrogation. A thorough investigation revealed the monk's ordination certificate, his robes, and begging bowl. The disciple said: "We had not yet reached thirty li from Guiyang when we came upon a large rock by the riverbank and rested beside it." Suddenly, the stone opened, and an old man called the monk inside. The rock then closed again. By dusk, when they waited for him to come out, he did not emerge, so we returned." In prison, the disciple laughed at his own absurd story and was severely interrogated. Eventually, he confessed to guilt; both disciples were sentenced to be executed by beheading. Several months later, the monk arrived in Guiyang. The family of the disciple petitioned for a retrial on grounds of wrongful conviction, and as a result, the officials involved were punished for their injustice. They questioned the monk, who indeed confirmed that he had entered the stone wall and met an old man. They spoke for a long time before exiting through a subterranean passage. He emerged in Taoyuan, Ding Zhou, and then slowly returned home while begging for food along the way. There are events like this; to this day, there are official records of the case preserved in the Guiyang Jian office.

27 古传剑侠甚著，近世寂不闻，先令人尝言常州张大卿一事，疑其剑侠也。云张买得婢，年三十馀，虽不艳丽，风骨语论，非凡物也。自挈一柳箱缄固，每戒人勿发。寻常十数日则失之，夜半后复从天窗中来，张心异之，不敢诘。岁馀生一女子，张意绸缪，俟其去，乃发箱视之，中藏一短剑及皂半臂，无他物，才归已觉，大怒曰：「奈何不听吾言！」取半臂披之，挥剑断其女头，倏然飞去，张急挽，已失所在。至今张氏祀于家祠，柳箱存焉。

Ancient accounts of swordsmen and chivalrous heroes were very prominent, but in recent times they have been completely unheard of. My late father once spoke of an incident involving Zhang Daqing from Changzhou, which I suspect was related to a sword 侠 (chivalric swordsman). He said that Zhang Daqing had bought a female servant who was over thirty years old. Although she was not strikingly beautiful, her bearing and eloquence marked her as no ordinary person. She carried with her a single willowwood chest tightly sealed, often warning people not to open it. After several days she would suddenly disappear, only to return at midnight through the skylight. Zhang was deeply puzzled but dared not question her. After more than a year, the woman gave birth to a daughter. Zhang, suspecting something unusual, waited until she left and then opened the chest. Inside were only a short sword and a black half-sleeve garment; there was nothing else. Just as he closed it, she returned and discovered what he had done. She became very angry and said: "How could you not listen to my words!" She took the half-sleeve garment and put it on, then brandished the sword and severed her daughter's head. In an instant, she flew away. Zhang rushed to stop her, but by then she had already vanished without a trace. To this day, the Zhang family honors her in their ancestral shrine, and the willowwood chest still remains there.

28 古传紫姑神，近世尤甚，宣和初禁之，乃绝。尝观其下神，用两手扶一筲箕，头插一箸，画灰盘作字，加笔于箸上，则能写纸，与人应答，自称「蓬莱大仙」，多女子也，有名字伯仲，作文可观，著棋则人无能敌者。馀寓南海，有一假儒衣冠者，能迎致其神，在书室中和馀诗云：「古书读尽到今书，不独才馀力有馀。自是丹山真凤子，太平呈瑞只须臾。」其人自不能文，疑有神助。然不识字人致之，则不能书，但以箸宛转画灰盘尔。此何理也？

Ancient accounts speak of Zigu Shen (the Purple Niece Deity), a practice that became especially prevalent in recent times. At the beginning of the Xuanhe era, it was banned and thus ceased entirely. I once observed how they summoned the deity: two hands supported a bamboo sieve, with one chopstick inserted in the head. They drew characters on an ash-covered plate and placed a brush on top of the chopstick; thus it could write on paper, responding to people's questions. It often referred to itself as "Penglai Daxian" (the Great Immortal of Penglai). Most of those who channeled were women, with names indicating seniority among siblings. Their writings were impressive, and if they played chess, no one could match their skill. While I was staying in Nanhai, there was a pretender dressed as a Confucian scholar who claimed to be able to summon the deity. In my study, he composed a poem with me: "Ancient books read through to modern ones, not only do I have talent but also abundant energy." From now on, the true phoenix son of Danshan, in times of peace and prosperity, presents omens of auspiciousness within moments." The man himself was not a literate person; it was suspected that he had divine assistance. Yet if someone who could not read or write summoned the spirit, it would be unable to write and could only move the chopstick to draw characters on the ash-covered plate. What is the explanation for this?

29 江南俗事神，疾病官事专求神，其巫不一，有号「香神」者，祠星辰，不用荤；有号「司徒神」者、「仙帝神」者，用牲，皆以酒为酌，名称甚多。尝于神堂中见仙帝神名位，有柴帝、郭帝、石帝、刘帝之号，盖五代周、晋、汉也，不知何故祀之，祀词并无义理。又以傀儡戏乐神，用禳官事，呼为弄戏。遇有系者，则许戏几棚。至赛时，张乐弄傀儡，初用楮钱，爇香启祷，犹如祠神。至弄戏，则秽谈群笑，无所不至。乡人聚观，饮酒醉，又殴击，往往因此又致讼系，许赛无已时。

In the Jiangnan region, it is customary to worship deities; when people suffer from illness or legal troubles, they exclusively seek help from these spirits. There are many kinds of shamans, among whom those called "Xiang Shen" (Incense Deity) worship stars and constellations without consuming any meat. Others known as "Situ Shen" (Minister Deity) or "Xian Di Shen" (Immortal Emperor Deity) do use animal sacrifices; all of them offer wine as libation. The names and titles are numerous indeed. I once saw in a shrine the names and titles of the "Xian Di Shen" (Immortal Emperor Deities), which included "Chai Di," "Guo Di," "Shi Di," and "Liu Di"—these were apparently from the Five Dynasties period, corresponding to the Zhou, Jin, and Han dynasties. I do not know why they are worshipped; their hymns of invocation contain no meaningful or rational content at all. They also entertain the deities with puppet shows, using them to appease official troubles, calling this performance "Nong Xi" (puppet play). When someone was in trouble or detained, they would promise the deities a certain number of puppet performances. When it came time to fulfill the vow, they would set up music and perform with puppets; at first, they burned paper money and lit incense to make offerings and prayers, just as in a deity's shrine. When it came to the puppet performance itself, they engaged in vulgar talk and loud laughter, going so far as to do anything without restraint. The local villagers gathered to watch, drank wine until they were drunk, and even fought with each other; such incidents often led to new lawsuits or detentions, resulting in endless vows for more performances.

30 张升侍中初监榷务，相传厅事有鬼物，官吏不敢宿直舍。张至，独寝厅上。夜半后，有物扪其足，如冰冷；须臾自足而上，循至顶复下，如此再四。张闭目引手持之，乃一毛臂甚巨，不敢视其状，但坚持之。闻鸡唱，忽作人语，初甚厉，已而渐逊，且言：「公官至侍中，语泄天机，自有阴祸，幸舍我。」张皆不恤，渐觉手中消铄，至晓都尽，怪遂绝。张每戒人云：「夜中但不开目，便不怖畏。」仲姊之夫先为张壻，亲为馀言不妄。

Zhang Sheng, the Attendant-in-Attendance (Shizhong), was initially in charge of a customs office. It was said that there were ghostly entities in the hall where official business was conducted, and thus officials dared not stay overnight in their assigned quarters. Zhang Zhi alone slept on the hall floor. After midnight, there was something that touched his feet, feeling as cold as ice; in a moment it moved upward from his feet to the top of his head and then back down again, repeating this several times. Zhang kept his eyes closed and reached out to grasp it; he found that it was a very large hairy arm, but dared not look at its appearance, merely holding on firmly. He heard a human voice speaking, initially very harsh, but gradually becoming more subdued. 31 熙宁癸丑，先公登第，天子擢居第一，为权臣所轧，故居第二，大父颇不平。湖州道场山有老僧，为大父言：「此非人事。道场山在州南离方，文笔山也，低于他州，故未有魁天下者。」僧乃丐缘，即山背建浮屠，望之如卓一笔。既成，语州人曰：「后三十年出状元。」大观贾安宅，政和莫俦，相继为廷试魁。此吾家事，非诞也。

My great-grandfather felt quite unjust about this. There was an old monk on Daochang Mountain in Huzhou who told my great-grandfather: "This is not a matter of human affairs." In the Daguan era, Jia Anzhai; in the Zhenghe era, Mo Chou—successively they became champions of the palace examination. This is a matter concerning my family; it is not a fabrication.

32 琼管四郡在海岛上，士人未尝有登第者。东坡责儋耳，与琼人姜唐佐游，喜其好学，与一联诗云：「沧海何尝断地脉，白袍端合破天荒。」东坡语姜云：「俟他日有验，当续成篇。」崇宁兴学，丕冒海隅，四郡士人亦向进，虽垦辟已久，恐卤瘠终无嘉谷尔。

The four commanderies under Qiongguan are located on an island; scholars there have never had anyone pass the imperial examination. Dongpo was exiled to Dan'er and traveled with Jiang Tangzuo, a native of Qiong. He appreciated the latter's love for learning and gave him a couplet of poetry: "The boundless sea has never severed the earth's veins; a white robe should indeed break new ground in the heavens." Dongpo told Jiang: "Wait until some day this proves true, and I will then complete the poem." In the Chongning era, education was promoted; thus, even in this remote coastal area, scholars from the four commanderies began to strive for advancement. Although the land had been cultivated for a long time, it was feared that its saline and barren soil would ultimately yield no good grain.

33 常州诸胡，馀外氏，自武平使枢密，宗愈继执政，宗回、宗师、宗炎、奕修皆两制，宗质四子同时作监司，家赀又高，东南号「富贵胡家」。相传祖茔三女山尤美，甚利子壻，馀母氏乃尊行，如渭阳诸壻，钱昂、黄辅国、李诗、柳廷俊、张巨、陈举、蒋存诚，皆为显官，馀无不出常调。吕吉甫太尉，自言其家不利女壻，不唯碌碌无用，如长倩馀中，成婚二十馀年，元佑初观望朝廷，上疏乞诛吕吉甫谢天下，后竟离婚。亦云祖茔三女山风水相刑也。馀表侄李熙嘏，狂生登第，吉甫以孙女妻之，自延安帅遣人纳吉，礼貌甚盛。熙嘏在京师，忽诣开封府投牒，愿离婚。蔡元长尹京，惊问所以，并无违律及不争财物，熙嘏但言平生不喜与「福建子」交涉，元长怒叱出，卒成婚。时人谓吕家风水已应。中州人每为闽人所窘，目为「福建子」，畏而憎之之辞。吉甫、元长皆闽人，故熙嘏戏之耳。

The Hu family of Changzhou, my maternal relatives, began with Wu Ping, who served as an envoy to the Military Council. Zong Yu succeeded him in a high official post; Zong Hui, Zong Shi, Zong Yan, and Yi Xiu all attained positions in the Two Secretariats (Liangzhi). Zong Zhi had four sons who simultaneously held supervisory posts as jiansi officials. Their family wealth was also substantial, and they were known throughout the southeast region as "the wealthy Hu family." It is said that the family's ancestral burial ground on Sanshun Mountain was especially beautiful, which greatly benefited their sons-in-law. My mother's side of the family belonged to this distinguished lineage; like the many well-connected sons-in-law from the Wei Yang region—Qian Ang, Huang Fuguo, Li Shi, Liu Tingjun, Zhang Ju, Chen Ju, and Jiang Cuncheng—all became prominent officials, while others were no less than normally expected in official appointments. Grand General Lü Jifu once claimed that his family was unlucky in sons-in-law, not only because they were mediocre and useless but also because of cases like Zhang Qianyu Zhong. After marrying for more than twenty years, at the beginning of Yuanyou he hesitated before the court and submitted a memorial requesting the execution of Lü Jifu to atone to the empire; eventually, their marriage was dissolved. It is also said that this misfortune stems from the geomantic inauspiciousness of the ancestral burial ground on Sanshu Mountain, where the fengshui elements were mutually harmful. My nephew by marriage, Li Xigua, was a reckless scholar who passed the examination. Lü Jifu gave his granddaughter in marriage to him. From his post as commander of Yan'an, he sent people to present auspicious gifts, and the ceremony was conducted with great pomp and courtesy. Xigua, while in the capital, suddenly went to Kaiyuan Prefecture Office and submitted a petition requesting divorce. Cai Yuanzhang, the governor of the capital, was startled and asked for the reason. There were no violations of law or disputes over property; Xigua merely stated that he had never liked associating with "Fujian sons." Yuanzhang angrily scolded him out, but in the end, the marriage was still completed. At that time, people said that the geomantic omen of the Lü family had already come to pass. People from Zhongzhou were often at a disadvantage when dealing with Fujian natives, who referred to them as "Fujiansons," a term expressing both fear and contempt. Lü Jifu and Cai Yuanzhang were both Fujian natives, so Xigua was merely teasing them with the term.

34 大父居湖州城西，绕宅为园，植果，有一李树实佳。家有姑，自幼时爱食，因占护，每李熟，他人莫敢采，家人号为「大姑李」，传其种于外。后数十年，诸父贫不能有祖构，而姑所嫁丁维为中大夫，典郡且富，遂售其地建宅，大姑尚无恙，竟得旧李。

My great-grandfather lived in the western part of Huzhou city, where he built a garden around his residence and planted fruit trees. Among them was a plum tree that bore particularly fine fruit. There was an aunt in the family who, since childhood, loved to eat its fruit. Because of this affection, she took special care of it; every time the plums ripened, no one else dared to pick them. The tree thus came to be called "Dagu Li" (Auntie's Plum). Its seeds were later propagated outside the family. Several decades later, my uncles were poor and could not maintain the ancestral house. However, the aunt who had married into the Ding family became Ding Wei, a Zhongdafa (Intermediate Counselor), governed a commandery, and grew wealthy. He then bought the land to build his residence. At that time, "Dagu" was still alive and eventually recovered the old plum tree.

35 王荆公妻越国吴夫人，性好洁成疾，公任真率，每不相合。自江宁乞骸归私第，有官藤床，吴假用未还，吏来索，左右莫敢言。公一旦跣而登床，偃仰良久，吴望见，即命送还。

Wang Jinggong's wife, Marquise Wu of Yueguo, had a strong preference for cleanliness to the point of obsession. Wang, who held an easygoing and straightforward manner in his conduct, often found himself at odds with her. When Wang Jinggong retired from office in Jiangning and returned to his private residence, there was an official rattan bed that Lady Wu had borrowed but not yet returned. When officials came to reclaim it, none of those around her dared to speak up. One day, Wang Jinggong suddenly went barefoot onto the bed and lay back on it for a long while; Lady Wu saw this and immediately ordered that the bed be returned.

36 荆公吴夫人有洁疾，其意不独恐污己，亦恐污人。长女之出，省之于江宁，夫人欣然裂绮縠制衣，将赠其甥，皆珍异也。忽有猫卧衣笥中，夫人即叱婢揭衣置浴室下，终不肯与人，竟腐败无敢取者。余大父至贫，挂冠月俸折支得压酒囊，诸子幼时，用为胫衣。先公痛念兹事，既显，尽以月俸颁昆弟宗族，终身不自吝一钱。诸父仰禄以活，不治生事。晚年迁谪，族人失俸，大有狼狈者，五叔父遂不聊生。余窃谓使荆公与大父易地，吴夫人安得有此疾！

Marquise Wu of Wang Jinggong had an obsession with cleanliness; her concern was not only about being soiled herself, but also about soiling others. When her eldest daughter went out to visit, visiting her in Jiangning, Lady Wu joyfully tore up brocade and gauze to make clothes, intending to give them to her niece; all were rare and precious fabrics. Suddenly, a cat was found lying in the chest of clothes. Lady Wu immediately scolded her servant to open the garments and place them beneath the bathhouse; she ultimately refused to give them to anyone. In the end, they rotted away, and no one dared take them. My great-grandfather was extremely poor; after retiring from office, his monthly salary in kind was converted into a wine press bag. When my uncles were young children, they used it as leg-warmers. My late father deeply remembered this matter; after attaining prominence, he distributed his entire monthly salary among his brothers and extended relatives, never sparing even a single coin for himself throughout his life. My uncles relied on their official salaries to survive, without engaging in any business or livelihood pursuits. In his later years, when my father was demoted and exiled, the clan lost their stipends, and many relatives were in dire straits; my fifth uncle was so distressed that he could no longer go on living. I secretly think that if Wang Jinggong and my great-grandfather had exchanged places, Lady Wu would never have developed such an obsession!

37 世传妇人有产鬼形者，不能执而杀之，则飞去，夜复归就乳，多瘁其母，俗呼为「旱魃」。亦分男女，女魃窃其家物以出，儿魃窃外物以归。初虞世和甫，名士善医，公卿争邀致，而性不可驯狎，往往尤急于权贵。每贵人求治病，则重诛求之，至于不可堪，所得赂旋以施贫者。最爱山谷黄庭坚，尝言：「山谷孝于亲，吾爱重之。」每得佳墨精楮奇玩，必归山谷。山谷尝语朝士：「初和甫于馀，正是一儿旱魃。」时坐中有素厌苦和甫者，率尔对曰：「到吾家便是女旱魃。」

It is commonly said that some women give birth to ghostly infants; if one cannot seize and kill them, they will fly away but return at night to nurse. These creatures often exhaust their mothers, and are popularly called "Hanba" (drought demon). They also come in male and female forms; a female Hanba steals items from the household to take away, while a male Hanba brings external objects back into the home. Chu Yu was a learned man and skilled physician; high-ranking officials vied to invite him, but his nature was difficult to tame or be familiar with, and he often treated powerful figures even more urgently. Whenever a nobleman sought his medical treatment, he would heavily demand payment, to the point of being unbearable; yet whatever bribes he received were soon given out to the poor. He most admired Huang Tingjian of Shangu Valley, once saying: "Huanggu Valley was filial to his parents; this is why I love and respect him." Whenever he acquired fine ink, high-quality paper, or rare curiosities, he would certainly give them to Huanggu Valley. Huang Tingjian once told his fellow court officials: "Chu Hefu's attitude toward me was exactly like that of a male Hanba (drought demon)." At the time, there were people present who had long been annoyed by Chu Hefu; one of them blurted out in reply: "If he came to my home, he would be a female Hanba."

38 崇宁铸九鼎，帝鼐居中，八鼎各镇一隅。是时行当十钱，苏州无赖子弟，冒法盗铸。会浙中大水，伶人对御作俳：「今岁东南大水，乞遣彤鼎往镇苏州。」或作鼎神附奏云：「不愿前去，恐一例铸作当十钱。」朝廷因治章綖之狱。

In the Chongning era (1102–1106), nine tripods were cast; the imperial tripod, Di Nai, was placed in the center, while the eight other tripods each stood to guard one corner. At that time, coins of ten times value were in circulation; in Suzhou, some idle and lawless youths violated the law by secretly casting counterfeit coins. At that time, a great flood struck Zhong region; an actor performed before the emperor with a humorous skit: "This year's great floods in the southeast—your Majesty should send the Vermilion Tripod to pacify Suzhou." Some portrayed a tripod deity as having attached himself and reported: "I do not wish to go, for I fear being cast into tenfold coins just like the rest." The imperial court thus proceeded with the investigation of Zhang Yan's case.

39 伶人丁先现者，在教坊数十年，每对御作俳，颇议正时事。尝在朝门与士大夫语曰：「先现衰老，无补朝廷也。」闻者哂之。

A performer named Ding Xianxian, who had spent several decades in the Jiaofang (Imperial Music Bureau), often performed humorous skits before the emperor and was quite bold in satirizing current affairs. He once said at the palace gate to a group of scholar-officials: "Ding Xianxian is old and frail, and can no longer be of use to the court." Those who heard this laughed at him.

40 王德用为使相，黑色，俗号「黑相」。尝与北使伴射，使已中的，黑相取箭焊头一发破前矢，俗号「劈筈箭」。姚麟亦善射，为殿帅十年，伴射常蒙奖赐。崇宁初，王恩以遭遇处位殿帅，不习弓矢，岁岁以伴射为窘。伶人对御作俳，先一人持一矢入，曰：「黑相劈筈箭，售钱三百万。」又一人持大矢入，曰：「老姚射不输箭，售钱三百万。」后二人挽箭一车入，曰：「车箭都卖一钱。」或问：「是何人家箭，价贱如此？」答曰：「王恩不及垛箭。」

Wang Deyong served as a shi xiang (a high-ranking official), and he had dark skin; the common people called him "Heixiang" ("Black Chancellor"). Once, while accompanying a northern envoy in an archery contest, the envoy had already hit the target. Heixiang took up an arrow, welded its tip, and shot it out to split the previous arrow—this became popularly known as "Pikua Jian" (the arrow that splits the fletching). Yao Lin was also an expert archer; he served as Commander of the Imperial Guards for ten years, and whenever he participated in archery contests with envoys, he often received imperial rewards. In the first year of Chongning, Wang En attained his position as Commander of the Imperial Guards through imperial favor, but he was not skilled in archery. Year after year, he found himself embarrassed and distressed by the requirement to participate in the archery contests with foreign envoys. A court performer performed a skit before the emperor, in which one actor entered holding an arrow and said: "The 'Pikua Jian' (arrow that splits the fletching) of Heixiang is for sale at three million coins." Another actor then entered holding a large arrow and said: "Old Yao's arrows never miss their mark; this one is also for sale at three million coins." Then two more actors dragged in a cart full of arrows and said: "All the arrows in this cart are for sale at one coin." Someone asked, "Whose family's arrows are these, so cheaply priced?" The reply was: "These are Wang En's arrows that never hit the target."

41 杨鼎臣大夫尝为馀言，绍圣间在成都，见提举茶马官，以课羡赐五品衣鱼。府中开宴，俳优口号有「茶牙人赐绯」之句，当时颇怒其妄发，亦笞之。小人中有冷眼，最不可欺。元符末，广帅柯述除直龙图阁，移知福州，训词有云：「延阁以待该博之士，傥践历中外，厥有成绩者，亦以命之。」柯无文采，颇不堪此「亦」字。

Doctor Yang Dingchen once told me that during the Shaosheng era, while he was in Chengdu, he saw an official in charge of tea and horse trade who received a five-rank fish-shaped badge (yuyu) as a reward for exceeding his quota. At an official banquet in the prefecture, a performer made a humorous remark that included the line: "The tea broker is granted a crimson badge." At the time, this was considered a bold and disrespectful exaggeration, and he was punished with caning. Among common people there are those with discerning eyes; they are the most difficult to deceive. In the final year of Yuanyou, Guangzhou's military governor Ke Shu was appointed as a Zhilongtugu official and transferred to serve as governor of Fuzhou. The imperial edict stated: "The Hall of Dragons is reserved for erudite scholars; if any have served both in court and abroad and achieved notable accomplishments, they too shall be so appointed." Ke Shu lacked literary talent and was quite unworthy of the word "yi" (also) in that statement.

42 熙宁间，王介甫行新法，欲用人材，或以选人为监司。赵济、刘谊皆雄州防御推官，提举常平等事，荐所部官改官，而举将自未改官。盖用才不限资格，又不欲便授品秩，且惜名器也。其时多引人上殿，伶人对上作俳，跨驴直登轩陛，左右止之，其人曰：「将谓有脚者尽上得。」荐者少沮。

In the Xining era, Wang Jiefu implemented the New Policies and wished to appoint capable individuals; sometimes he appointed xuanren (lower-ranking officials) as jianshi (supervisory officials). Zhao Ji and Liu Yi were both Touguan (judicial officials) in Xiongzhou, responsible for roles such as overseeing the Pingchang Equalization Bureau. They recommended officials under their jurisdiction to be promoted or reassigned, yet they themselves had not yet received a change in rank or position. This was because the use of talent was not restricted by qualifications, and at the same time there was no desire to immediately grant official ranks; it was also a way to cherish and preserve the dignity of titles. At that time, many people were summoned to the palace. A court performer performed a skit for the emperor in which he rode a donkey straight up onto the imperial steps. Those on the side tried to stop him, but the performer said: "I thought anyone with legs could ascend here." Those who recommended others were somewhat discouraged.

43 文及甫，潞公子也，二十八岁，以直龙图阁知陕州，士论少之。郡僚戏云：「本州岛公筵，客将司奉台旨吃炒剥。」当时传以为笑。

Wen Jifu was the son of Lu Gong; at twenty-eight years old, he was appointed as Zhilongtugu and governor of Shanzhou. Scholars and officials generally looked down on him. The local officials joked: "At this prefecture's banquet, the guests will be ordered by your honor to eat stir-fried and peeled snacks." This was widely circulated at the time as a source of amusement.

44 钱遹田家子，高科膴仕，性甚鲁。每遇失汗，则负重走斋中，汗出乃苏。既为禁从，犹如此，或取十馀千钱，就帐内荷之以作力。诸方不载此法，但人生恶安逸、喜劳动，惜乎非中庸也。轻薄子以为此出汗方，编入御药院，可一笑，故记之。

Qian Yu was the son of a wealthy family; he passed high-level examinations and held prestigious official posts, but his nature was very dull and unintelligent. Whenever he suffered from a fainting spell due to loss of body fluids, he would carry heavy objects and run around his study; only after sweating profusely would he regain consciousness. Even after becoming a palace attendant, he still behaved this way; sometimes he would take ten thousand coins, carry them in his tent to exert himself through physical labor. This method is not recorded in any medical texts; yet, it seems that some people naturally dislike idleness and prefer physical labor. Unfortunately, this behavior deviates from the principle of moderation. Light-minded individuals regarded this as a method for inducing perspiration and included it in the records of the Imperial Pharmaceutical Bureau, which is quite laughable; thus I have recorded it here.

45 元佑间有大臣，不欲书名氏。父尝贬死朱崖，寓柩不归。既贵，自过海迎取。已更数十年，无识其父柩者，于僧房中有数棺，枯骨无款记，不获已乃挈一棺归，与其母合葬。后竞传误取僧骨来。绍圣初，言者欲萋斐，以无验不敢举。

In the Yuanyou era, there was a high-ranking official who did not wish to record his name and surname. His father had once been demoted and died in Zhuyi, where the coffin was left in temporary storage without being returned home. After attaining high rank, he personally crossed the sea to retrieve it. After several decades had passed, no one recognized the coffin of his father. In a monks' quarters there were several coffins containing dry bones with no inscriptions or records; having no other choice, he took one coffin and returned it, burying it together with his mother. Later, rumors spread that he had mistakenly taken a monk's bones. In the first year of the Shaosheng era, those who wished to accuse him with false charges found no evidence and thus dared not proceed.

46 杭州繁华，部使者多在州置司，各有公帑。州倅二员，都厅公事分委诸曹，倅号无事，日陪使府外台宴饮。东坡倅杭，不胜杯酌，诸公钦其才望，朝夕聚首，疲于应接，乃号杭倅为「酒食地狱」。后袁毂倅杭，适与郡将不协，诸司缘此亦相疏，袁语所亲曰：「酒食地狱，正值狱空。」传以为笑。

Hangzhou is a prosperous city; many officials from central government departments established their offices in the prefecture, each maintaining public funds. There were two assistant magistrates of the prefecture; official matters in the main hall were divided and delegated to various departments. The assistants, therefore, had little actual work to do each day, spending their time accompanying the provincial officials at banquets and social gatherings outside the government office. Dongpo, serving as assistant magistrate of Hangzhou, could not bear the constant drinking. The officials greatly admired his talent and reputation, gathering with him morning and evening, exhausting him with social obligations. Thus, he referred to the position of Hangzhou assistant magistrate as "the Hell of Banquets and Feasts." Later, Yuan Gu served as assistant magistrate of Hangzhou. He happened to be at odds with the local military commander; because of this, relations between various offices also became strained. Yuan remarked to his close associates: "The Hell of Banquets and Feasts—just when it is exactly empty." This story was passed on as a source of amusement.

47 苏州李章，以口舌为生计，介甫集有《李章下第》诗，亦才子也。尝游湖州，人皆厌其乞索。曾诣富人曹监簿家，曹方剖嘉鱼，闻其来，遽匿鱼出对之，章已入耳目。既坐，曹与论文，不及他事，冀其速去，谈及介甫《字说》，章因言：「世俗讹谬用字，如本乡苏州，篆文鱼在禾左，隶书鱼在禾右，不知何等小子，移过此鱼。」曹拊掌，共匕箸。

Li Zhang of Suzhou made a living through eloquence and rhetoric; there is a poem titled "Li Zhang Fails the Imperial Examination" in Jiefu's collection, indicating that he was also a man of talent. He once traveled to Huzhou, where people all disliked his constant begging and demands for money or gifts. He once visited the home of Cao Jianbu, a wealthy man in Huzhou. At that time, Cao was preparing a rare Jia fish; upon hearing of Li Zhang's arrival, he hastily hid the fish and went out to receive him, but Zhang had already seen everything with his own eyes. Once seated, Cao engaged him in a discussion on literature, deliberately avoiding other topics, hoping he would leave quickly. When the conversation touched upon Ouyang Jiefu's "Explanation of Characters," Zhang remarked: "The common people mistakenly use characters; for example, in our native Suzhou, the seal script character for 'fish' is placed to the left of 'grain,' while in clerical script it appears on the right. I wonder which ignorant person moved this fish over." Cao clapped his hands with laughter, and together they resumed their meal with chopsticks and serving spoons.

48 昔有郭巨公进建第，落成日，设诸匠列坐于子弟右。或以为不可，巨公指诸匠曰：「此造屋者。」又指其子弟曰：「此卖屋者，固自有序。」识者以为名言，可为破家子戒。

In the past, there was Guo Ju Gong who built a new residence; on the day it was completed, he arranged for all the craftsmen to sit in rows to the right of his sons and nephews. Some thought this inappropriate, but Ju Gong pointed at the craftsmen and said: "These are the ones who built the house." He then pointed to his sons and nephews and said: "These are the ones who sold the house; naturally, there is a proper order." Those with insight regarded these words as wise remarks, which could serve as a warning to wasteful heirs.

49 常州苏掖，仕至监司，家富甚啬。每置产，吝不与直，争一钱至失色。尤喜乘人窘急，时以微资取奇货。尝买别墅，与售者反复甚苦，其子在旁曰：「大人可少增金，我辈他日卖之，亦得善价也。」父愕然，自是少悟。士大夫竞传其语。

Su Ye of Changzhou rose in officialdom to the rank of supervisory commissioner and was extremely wealthy yet very stingy. Whenever he purchased property, he was miserly and reluctant to pay a fair price, often turning pale over disputes over even one coin. He especially enjoyed taking advantage of others in times of distress, using minimal funds to acquire rare or valuable goods. He once bought a villa, and after prolonged and bitter haggling with the seller, his son, standing by, said: "Father, you might consider adding a little more money; when we sell it in the future, we will also get a good price." The father was startled and from then on somewhat came to understand. Scholars and officials widely passed on this saying.

50 钱塘郎忠厚，游当涂诸公间，颇稔熟，好叙亲旧，见势位无不纳拜者。至人失势，则相疏。时人目之为「富贵亲情」。

Lang Zhonghou of Qiantang traveled among the officials in Dangtu and became quite well-acquainted with them; he was fond of recounting family ties and old acquaintances, and to no one of power or position did he fail to offer a bow of respect. When those in power lost their influence, however, they became distant toward him. Contemporaries nicknamed him "Wealthy Kinship."

51 润州一监征，与务胥盗官钱，皆藏之胥家，约曰：「官满分以装我。」胥伪诺之。既代去，卒不与一钱，监征不敢索，悒悒渡扬子江，竟卒于维扬。胥得全贿，遂富，告归治田宅。是年妻孕，如见监徵褰帏而入，即诞子，甚慧。长喜书，胥使之就学。二十岁登第，胥大喜，尽鬻其产，挈家至京师，为桂玉费。其子调官南下，已匮乏，至维扬病亡。胥无所归，贫索无聊，悔悟而卒。

A certain supervisor of revenue in Runzhou colluded with a clerk to steal official funds, hiding all the money at the clerk's home. They made an agreement: "When my term is over, we will divide it and use it for our expenses." The clerk pretended to agree. After his term ended and he was replaced, the clerk gave him not a single coin; the supervisor of revenue dared not demand it, and passed through Yangzi River in gloomy dejection, eventually dying in Weiyang. The clerk thus retained the entire bribe and became wealthy, returning home to purchase land and build a residence. That same year, his wife was pregnant; as if seeing the supervisor of revenue draw aside the curtain and enter, she gave birth to a son who proved very intelligent. The boy showed an early fondness for reading and writing, so the clerk sent him to school. At twenty he passed the imperial examination; the clerk was overjoyed, sold all his property, brought his family to the capital city, and spent the money on expenses for his son's official career. The clerk's son was assigned an official post in the south but had already fallen into financial hardship, and he died of illness upon arriving at Weiyang. The clerk had nowhere to return; destitute and wretched, he eventually repented in regret and died.

52 赵廷臣故渝州洞蛮，与诸酋约降朝廷。至洞，赵乃率诸酋杀之，扬言众叛，掩以为己功，又尽得其财物。故廷臣世赀高，筮仕被擢用。生子谂，少年及第，几为殿魁；未三十岁，升朝为国子博士，忽以狂逆伏法。廷臣自河东提刑配琼州，母、妻、妹分配岭外，家赀没官。识者谓谂等乃诸洞酋后身。

Zhao Tingchen was originally a native of Yuzhou Dong 蛮 (Dong ethnic group), who made an agreement with various tribal chieftains to surrender to the imperial court. When they arrived at the Dong territory, Zhao led the chieftains in killing them, publicly declared a mass rebellion, and claimed it as his own achievement, while also seizing all their possessions. Therefore, the family of Tingchen became very wealthy; upon entering officialdom, he was quickly promoted and entrusted with important posts. He had a son named Zhan, who passed the imperial examination at a young age and was nearly ranked first in the palace examination; before reaching thirty years of age, he was promoted to serve as a Guizi Doctor (Doctor of the Imperial Academy), but suddenly fell into madness and lawlessness, eventually being executed for his crimes. Tingchen was demoted from the position of Tixing (Inspector of Justice) in Hedong to Qiongzhou, while his mother, wife, and sister were exiled beyond the Lingnan region. All family assets were confiscated by the government. Those with insight said that Zhan and others were the reincarnations of the descendants of the Dong chieftains.

53 沈括存中，入翰苑，出塞垣，为闻人。晚娶张氏，悍虐，存中不能制，时被棰骂，捽须堕地，儿女号泣而拾之，须上有血肉者，又相与号恸，张终不恕。馀仲姊嫁其子清直，张出也。存中长子博毅，前妻儿，张逐出之。存中时往賙给，张知辄怒，因诬长子凶逆暗昧事，存中责安置秀州。张时时步入府中，诉其夫子，家人辈徒跣从劝于道。先公闻之，颇怜仲姊，乃夺之归宗。存中投闲十馀年，绍圣初复官，领宫祠。张忽病死，人皆为存中贺，而存中恍惚不安。船过扬子江，遂欲投水，左右挽持之，得无患，未几不禄。或疑平日为张所苦，又在患难，方幸相脱，乃尔何耶？余以为此妇妬暴，非碌碌者，虽死魂魄犹有凭借。

Shen Kuo Cunzhong entered the Hanyuan (Hanlin Academy) and served on border outposts, becoming a well-known figure. In his later years, Shen Cunzhong married a woman of the Zhang family, who was violent and cruel. Cunzhong could not control her; he often suffered beatings and verbal abuse at her hands. She even pulled out his beard until it fell to the ground. His children wept as they picked it up piece by piece—some strands still bearing blood and flesh—and together they wailed in grief, but Lady Zhang never showed mercy or forgiveness. My elder sister-in-law married his son Qingzhi, who was a son of Lady Zhang. Cunzhong's eldest son, Boyi, had children from his first marriage; these were driven out by Lady Zhang. Cunzhong often went to provide financial support, but whenever Lady Zhang learned of this, she became furious. She then falsely accused her eldest son of violent and dishonorable conduct, leading Cunzhong to be held responsible and ordered to be placed under house arrest in Xiuzhou. Lady Zhang frequently entered the government office, lodging complaints against her husband and father-in-law; members of his family followed along barefoot on the road, pleading with her to desist. My late father heard about this and felt great sympathy for my elder sister-in-law; he then took action to have her returned to her original family. Cunzhong was left idle for more than ten years, but in the first year of Shaosheng he was reinstated to office and appointed to oversee a palace shrine. Lady Zhang suddenly fell ill and died; everyone congratulated Cunzhong, but he appeared dazed and uneasy. When his boat passed Yangzi River, he suddenly wanted to drown himself; those around him held him back and prevented the act. Soon after, he died without attaining further official rank or fortune. Some people wondered: if in daily life he had been tormented by Lady Zhang, and now that she was gone and they were freed from hardship, why did he still behave this way? I believe this woman was jealous and violent, not an ordinary person; even after her death, her spirit still exerted some influence.

54 胡宗甫妻张氏，极妬。元丰中官京局，母氏常过其家。有小婢云英行酒，与主人相顾而笑，张见而嫌之。婢亦觉，是夕，自缢于厕。家人惊告，张饮嚼自如。母氏不遑处，乃归。明年，张之爱女病，作婢语责张曰：「我由尔死，尚未足道；既闻之，饮食笑乐安忍耶？必令主死，尔诸子继之，使尔孑然无聊，以偿我昔痛！」未几，宗甫捐馆，张遽出京还常州，三子尽亡，姑妇四人孀居。张晚年病发，宛转哀鸣，求诸婢餔饲扶掖，或责以前事，则流涕无语，如是十馀年乃卒。

Zhang Shi, the wife of Hu Zongfu, was extremely jealous. In the Yuang 丰 era, he served in a government bureau in the capital; his mother often visited their home. A young female servant named Yunying was serving wine, and she exchanged glances with the master of the house and smiled; Lady Zhang saw this and became suspicious. The maid also realized what had happened; that same night, she hanged herself in the latrine. When the family members rushed to inform her of the incident, Lady Zhang continued eating and drinking as if nothing had happened. Lady Mu was greatly distressed and could not remain there any longer; she returned home in haste. The following year, Lady Zhang's beloved daughter fell ill and began speaking as if in the voice of a servant girl, scolding her mother: "I died because of you; that alone is not yet enough to speak of. Having heard what happened, how could you still eat, drink, and laugh with such ease?" She must surely cause the master to die; then your sons will follow. This will leave you utterly alone and wretched, thus repaying me for my past suffering!" Not long after, Zongfu died; Lady Zhang hurriedly left the capital and returned to Changzhou. All three of her sons perished, leaving four women—her daughter-in-law and mother-in-law—widowed and living in solitude. In her later years, Lady Zhang fell ill and writhed in agony, wailing pitifully. She begged the female servants to feed and support her; if anyone reproached her for past deeds, she would weep silently without a word. Thus it continued for more than ten years until she finally died.

55 王韶在熙河，多杀伐。晚年知洪州，学佛，一日问长老祖心曰：「昔未闻道，罪障固多，今闻道矣，罪障灭乎？」心曰：「今有人，贫负债，及富贵而债主至，还否？」韶曰：「必还。」曰：「然则闻道矣，奈债主不相放何耶！」未几，疽发于脑卒。

Wang Shao, while in Xihedao, engaged in many acts of killing and warfare. In his later years, Wang Shao served as the magistrate of Hongzhou and took up Buddhism. One day he asked the elder monk Zuxin: "In my past life, before I heard the Dharma, I certainly committed many sins and accumulated heavy karmic obstacles. Now that I have come to understand the Dharma, do these sins and obstacles vanish?" Zuxin replied: "Suppose there is a person who was poor and in debt; when he later becomes wealthy and the creditors come to collect, will he repay them?" Shao said: "He must repay them." Zuxin said: "Then, even after hearing the Dharma, how can you escape the debt collectors?"! Not long afterward, he died from a carbuncle that broke out on his head.

59 绵州杨鼎臣，年十馀岁，所生母死，殡菜园中。后十年登第，调官，欲积俸营葬，凡两任，不能办。后改官知彭州九陇县，升朝为安倅，追赠所生邑号，方获襄事。杨每惧微时草率，棺衾不如法。既彻面衣若生，衣装俨然，盖已三十年。杨抱持恸绝，奉尸易衣而葬，观者感叹，诚孝之报如此。

Yang Dingchen of Mianzhou, when he was only more than ten years old, lost his birth mother, who was buried in a vegetable garden. Ten years later, he passed the imperial examination and was assigned an official post; wishing to save his salary for a proper reburial of his mother, he served through two appointments but still could not raise enough funds. Later, when he was transferred to serve as the magistrate of Jiulong Xian in Pengzhou and promoted to a court official serving as an assistant governor (An Cui), he posthumously bestowed a title upon his birth mother with the designation of her native county. Only then did he finally manage to carry out the proper funeral rites for her. Yang often feared that in his humble early years, his mother had been buried in a careless manner, with a coffin and shrouds that did not meet proper funeral standards. When the outer layers of cloth covering her face were removed, she appeared as if still alive; her clothing and appearance remained intact, a full thirty years later. Yang clung to his mother's body in grief so intense that he fainted, then respectfully dressed her in new clothes before reburial. Those who witnessed the scene were deeply moved; such was the reward of sincere filial piety.

61 崇宁初，姚舜仁献明堂议，以秘书少监修建明堂，专掌制度。姚议太室用茅覆，尊尧制也，竟不成。政和初，睿断天成，遂建合宫之制，不用茅，可见姚论之迂。亲祠北郊，自祖宗以来不得定议，议者多曰：「天子祭天地，大裘而冕。」传云：「大裘，黑羔裘也。」夏至极暑，至尊御羔裘不便，遂中辍。政和初，始定夏祭之礼。圣人之于天道，宜自得之。

In the first year of Chongning, Yao Shunren submitted proposals regarding the construction of the Mingtang (Hall of Bright Government). Appointed as Assistant Secretary of the Secretariat in charge of building the Mingtang, he was solely responsible for its design and regulations. Yao's proposal to cover Tai Shi (the main hall) with dried reeds followed the model of Emperor Yao, but it was ultimately not carried out. In the first year of Zhenghe, an imperial decision was made to construct the Hegong (Hall of Unity) system without using reeds, demonstrating how outdated and impractical Yao's argument had been. The issue of personally performing sacrifices at the northern suburbs, a matter that had remained unresolved since the ancestors and emperors of previous generations, was often discussed. Many commentators argued: "The Son of Heaven offers sacrifices to heaven and earth in the grand fur robe and ceremonial cap." Classical texts state: "The grand fur robe is made from black kid leather." On the summer solstice, when it was extremely hot, it was inconvenient for the Son of Heaven to wear a garment of kid leather; thus, the ceremony was suspended. In the first year of Zhenghe (1111–1118), the ritual for summer sacrifices was finally established. Sages, in their understanding of the principles of heaven, should attain them through their own insight.
